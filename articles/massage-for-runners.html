Massage for Runners

How therapeutic massage may support comfort, recovery, mobility, and consistent training throughout a runner's season.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Running is beautifully simple: one foot in front of the other, repeated again and again. But that repetition also means the body experiences thousands of loading cycles during a single training session.

Calves work to control and propel the body. The hips help stabilize each step. Hamstrings, quadriceps, glutes, feet, and trunk muscles coordinate to keep the runner moving efficiently. As mileage, speed, hills, or training frequency increase, those tissues may begin to feel tired, stiff, or sore.

Massage cannot replace appropriate training, sleep, nutrition, strength work, or medical care. It can, however, be one useful part of a broader recovery plan.

Start Here

The Quick Answer

Massage may help runners feel less sore, more relaxed, and temporarily more comfortable moving.

Its strongest role is generally recovery, comfort, and short-term flexibility rather than directly improving running performance. The best session is not automatically the deepest one.

Recovery is part of training—not something that happens only when training stops.
Running Mechanics

What Running Asks of the Body

Running repeatedly asks muscles and connective tissues to absorb force, stabilize joints, store and release energy, and produce forward movement.

Lower Leg

Calves

The gastrocnemius and soleus contribute to ankle control and push-off. Hills, speed work, footwear changes, and increased mileage can make the calves feel especially fatigued.

Front of Thigh

Quadriceps

The quadriceps help control the knee and absorb load, particularly during downhill running and deceleration.

Back of Thigh

Hamstrings

The hamstrings contribute to hip extension, knee control, and movement of the leg throughout the running cycle.

Pelvis & Hip

Glutes & Hips

The gluteal and surrounding hip muscles help stabilize the pelvis and control the leg while the runner is supported on one foot.

Ground Contact

Feet & Lower Legs

The muscles of the feet and lower legs continually respond to terrain, speed, footwear, surface, and impact.

Stability

Trunk

Running also requires coordinated trunk and pelvic control. Fatigue is not limited to the legs.

Running itself is not inherently damaging.

The body is designed to adapt to appropriate physical stress. Problems are more likely when training demands increase faster than the body can comfortably adapt.

Recovery

What Massage May Help With

Massage does not need to force muscles into a different shape or “correct” every perceived imbalance to be useful.

Post-Exercise Soreness

Massage may help reduce the perception of delayed muscle soreness after strenuous or unfamiliar exercise.

Muscle Comfort

Tired or overworked areas may feel more comfortable after appropriately dosed bodywork.

Short-Term Mobility

Some runners notice temporary improvements in flexibility or ease of movement after a session.

Relaxation

Training can be mentally demanding as well as physical. Massage can create intentional downtime.

Body Awareness

A session can provide time to notice areas that feel unusually sensitive, tired, or restricted.

Recovery Routine

Scheduled massage can create a deliberate recovery checkpoint during demanding training periods.

Common Myth

Does Massage Need to “Flush Out” Your Muscles?

No.

Runners sometimes hear that massage is necessary to remove lactic acid, flush toxins, or mechanically squeeze waste products out of muscles.

Those explanations oversimplify how the body works. Lactate produced during exercise is processed relatively quickly through normal physiology.

The useful effects of massage are better understood through comfort, sensation, relaxation, temporary changes in movement, and the overall experience of recovery.

Timing Matters

Before the Run vs. After the Run

Massage should match where you are in your training cycle. A pre-race session and a post-race recovery session should not feel identical.

Before Running

Keep It Familiar & Comfortable

  • Keep pressure comfortable.
  • Avoid techniques likely to create soreness.
  • Favor shorter, lighter, or stimulating work.
  • Pair massage with the runner's usual warm-up.
  • Avoid experimenting with unfamiliar techniques on race day.
After Running

Recovery First

  • Walk comfortably after finishing.
  • Replace fluids and food appropriately.
  • Change into dry, comfortable clothing.
  • Prioritize sleep and rest.
  • Use gentle movement as tolerated.
The closer you are to an important race, the less interested you should be in discovering how much pressure your legs can tolerate.
Common Questions

Areas Runners Commonly Ask About

Calf Tightness

Calf fatigue is common after hills, speed sessions, footwear changes, or increased mileage. Sudden swelling or unexplained severe calf pain should not simply be massaged.

Hamstring Tightness

Hamstrings may feel stiff after demanding workouts. Persistent focal pain, weakness, bruising, or symptoms following a sudden pull deserve further evaluation.

Hip Tightness

Massage may improve comfort around the hips and glutes, but strengthening and training decisions may matter more when symptoms repeatedly return.

IT Band Complaints

The iliotibial band is strong connective tissue. It does not need to be aggressively “rolled out” or painfully compressed.

Foot Fatigue

Foot and lower-leg massage can feel restorative after prolonged running, but persistent heel or arch pain may require more than massage alone.

Knee Discomfort

Massage does not directly repair knee structures. Work on surrounding muscles may improve comfort, but ongoing knee pain deserves appropriate assessment.

The Bigger Picture

Can Massage Prevent Running Injuries?

It is tempting to think of massage as preventive maintenance: keep the muscles loose and injuries will not happen.

The reality is more complicated. Running injuries can be influenced by training load, tissue capacity, previous injury, strength, recovery, sleep, terrain, footwear, biomechanics, and many other factors.

Massage may support recovery, but it should not be treated as an injury-prevention guarantee.

Consistent training, sensible progression, adequate recovery, strength work, and responding appropriately to persistent symptoms remain important.

Pressure

Do Runners Need Deep Tissue Massage?

No.

Some runners enjoy firm therapeutic work. Others respond better to moderate pressure. The appropriate session depends on timing, goals, sensitivity, recent training, and how the tissues respond.

More pressure does not automatically mean a more effective treatment.

If you are bracing, holding your breath, or tightening your entire leg, reducing the pressure may allow more productive work.
Scheduling

How Often Should Runners Get Massage?

There is no medically required massage schedule for runners. Frequency depends on training volume, goals, preference, budget, and individual response.

Recreational Running

Some runners schedule massage monthly or when training volume increases.

Higher-Volume Training

Runners in heavier training blocks may prefer sessions every two to four weeks, depending on individual recovery needs.

Race Preparation

Sessions can be scheduled around long runs, recovery days, or taper periods rather than following a rigid calendar.

After an Event

Timing and pressure should reflect how the athlete feels rather than assuming every tired muscle needs immediate deep work.

Whole-Person Recovery

Build Recovery Around More Than Massage

Sleep

Consistent sleep supports physical recovery and training adaptation.

Nutrition

Training creates energy demands that require adequate food and nutrients.

Hydration

Fluid needs vary with duration, environment, and individual sweat rate.

Strength

Muscles and connective tissues benefit from adequate capacity, not simply from being stretched or massaged.

Easy Days

Every training day does not need to be difficult. Variation helps manage accumulated load.

Progression

Sudden increases in mileage, hills, speed, or frequency can challenge tissue tolerance.

Before Your Session

What Should You Tell Your Massage Therapist?

  • What distance are you currently training for?
  • When was your last hard workout or race?
  • When is your next important run?
  • Have you recently increased mileage, hills, or intensity?
  • Are any areas painful rather than simply sore?
  • Have you had previous running injuries?
  • What pressure usually feels productive to you?
  • Is your goal relaxation, general recovery, or focused work on a specific area?

When Massage Should Wait

Running soreness and injury are not the same thing. Massage should not be used to push through symptoms that need medical evaluation.

Seek appropriate healthcare guidance for suspected fracture, significant swelling after injury, inability to bear weight normally, new numbness or significant weakness, open wounds, active infection, fever, or severe or rapidly worsening pain.

Unexplained one-sided calf swelling, warmth, redness, or significant tenderness requires prompt medical evaluation rather than massage.

Bottom Line

The Takeaway

Running asks the body to tolerate repeated load, and recovery is part of the training process.

Massage may help reduce perceived soreness, improve comfort, temporarily support flexibility, and create intentional recovery time.

It does not replace intelligent training, strength work, sleep, nutrition, hydration, or medical care when needed.

The best massage for a runner is not necessarily the deepest session. It is the session that matches the athlete's goals, training schedule, current condition, and individual response.

Train with intention. Recover with intention. Give your body enough time and support to adapt to the work you are asking it to do.
