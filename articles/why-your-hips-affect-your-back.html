Why Your Hips Affect Your Back

How the hips, pelvis, trunk, movement habits, strength, and muscle tension can influence low-back comfort—and where massage may help.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

The hips and lower back do not work as separate systems. They share movement, load, muscular support, and the pelvis that connects the trunk to the legs.

When the hips feel stiff, weak, overworked, or painful, the lower back may take on more effort during walking, lifting, sitting, climbing stairs, exercising, or changing positions.

That does not mean every case of back pain begins in the hips—but the relationship is important enough to consider.

The Hip–Back Connection

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint designed to provide both stability and a wide range of movement. The lumbar spine and pelvis help the trunk bend, rotate, transfer force, and stay balanced over the legs.

During everyday movement, the hips and lower back constantly share responsibility.

Not a One-Way Relationship

Hip limitations may influence the back, and back symptoms may also change how someone uses the hips. Pain, fear, fatigue, sleep, and activity all affect the pattern.

How the Hips May Change Back Demand

This is one possible movement pattern—not a diagnosis or a rule that applies to every person.

Step One

Hip Motion Feels Limited

Bending, rotating, extending the leg, or moving into a squat may feel restricted or uncomfortable.

Step Two

Movement Shifts Elsewhere

The pelvis, lower back, knees, or feet may contribute more to complete the task.

Step Three

Back Muscles Work Harder

Trunk muscles may increase effort to stabilize, lift, or manage the altered movement.

Step Four

Fatigue or Guarding Develops

The back or hips may begin to feel tight, tired, sore, or difficult to relax.

Step Five

Movement Becomes More Protective

Pain or concern may lead to less movement variety, creating even more stiffness or loss of confidence.

What Parts of the Hip Region Matter?

Several joints and muscle groups contribute to hip movement and pelvic control.

Hip Flexors

These muscles help lift the thigh and control hip extension. They may feel overworked after prolonged sitting, running, climbing, or repeated leg lifting.

Gluteal Muscles

The glutes help produce and control hip extension, rotation, and side-to-side stability during walking, lifting, and standing.

Deep Hip Rotators

Smaller muscles around the hip help guide rotation and stabilize the joint during movement.

Adductors and Inner Thigh

These muscles help control the leg and pelvis during walking, balance, and changes of direction.

Hamstrings

The hamstrings cross the hip and knee and may influence how someone bends, walks, runs, or lifts.

Pelvis and Trunk

The abdominal, spinal, and pelvic muscles help transfer force between the upper body and the legs.

Common Situations Where the Connection Shows Up

These patterns can have many causes and should not be used to diagnose yourself.

After Long Sitting

The hips may feel stiff while the lower back becomes tired from staying in one position. Standing and changing positions may initially feel awkward.

During Squats or Lifting

Limited hip motion, reduced strength, fear, fatigue, or task technique may increase how much movement or force is managed by the back.

While Walking or Running

Hip extension, rotation, strength, stride, footwear, training load, and fatigue may all affect how forces move through the pelvis and spine.

When Climbing Stairs

Hip and leg strength help raise and stabilize the body. When those tasks feel difficult, the trunk may lean or brace more.

After an Injury or Break

Pain, reduced activity, illness, surgery, or time away from exercise may change strength, mobility, and movement confidence.

How Massage Can Support the Hip–Back Relationship

Massage can be a valuable part of a broader plan for comfort, mobility, recovery, and body awareness—especially when the hips, glutes, thighs, and lower back feel overworked or guarded.

Ease Muscular Tension

Massage may help reduce the sense of tightness through the glutes, hip rotators, thighs, and lower back.

Improve Movement Comfort

When tissues feel less guarded, walking, stretching, exercise, and daily movement may feel easier and more approachable.

Support Recovery

Massage can complement exercise, physical therapy, mobility work, rest, and gradual return to activity.

Increase Body Awareness

A session may help you notice gripping, breath-holding, uneven weight shifting, or areas that work harder than expected.

Encourage Relaxation

Stress and pain can increase muscular guarding. Therapeutic touch may help the body feel safer using less unnecessary effort.

Create Consistent Care

Regular massage can become part of a sustainable routine rather than waiting until discomfort becomes severe.

A More Complete Hip-and-Back Support Plan

The strongest plan usually combines symptom relief with movement, strength, recovery, and appropriate healthcare.

Comfort

Use Massage and Recovery Strategically

Massage, heat when appropriate, sleep, pacing, and comfortable movement may help reduce guarding and improve short-term comfort.

Mobility

Practice Useful Range of Motion

Work on movements your life requires rather than forcing an arbitrary flexibility goal.

Strength

Build Capacity Gradually

Hip, leg, and trunk strengthening can help the body tolerate walking, lifting, stairs, work, and exercise.

Environment

Change Positions and Tasks

Reduce long periods in one position and adjust chairs, work surfaces, training volume, or lifting setup when possible.

Guidance

Use Rehabilitation When Needed

A physical therapist or other qualified professional can help assess persistent symptoms and build an individualized program.

Consistency

Support the Body Before a Flare

Regular movement, recovery, and massage may be more useful than responding only after discomfort becomes intense.

A Practical Movement Check

  • Do your hips feel different from side to side during a task?
  • Does your back tighten after sitting for a long time?
  • Do stairs, squats, walking, or lifting change your symptoms?
  • Are you holding your breath or bracing harder than necessary?
  • Does gentle movement help more than staying still?
  • Would massage help you feel more comfortable beginning movement?

What Massage Cannot Do Alone

Massage cannot diagnose the source of hip or back pain, repair a fracture, reverse arthritis, restore lost strength, or replace rehabilitation when a structured exercise plan is needed.

Its value is not limited by those boundaries. Massage can still be a meaningful part of care by helping with comfort, tension, recovery, relaxation, and readiness to move.

When to Seek Medical Care

Seek prompt evaluation for severe pain after a fall or injury, inability to bear weight, visible deformity, fever with hip or back pain, new weakness or numbness, loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or saddle region, unexplained weight loss, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Persistent pain that interferes with walking, sleep, work, or daily activity also deserves professional assessment.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

The hips and back function as part of one connected movement system.

Massage can help create comfort and reduce muscular guarding, making it easier to participate in movement, exercise, work, and recovery.

The goal is not to force perfect mobility. The goal is to help you move with more options, less unnecessary effort, and greater confidence.
