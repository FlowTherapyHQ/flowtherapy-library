How Often Should You Get Up From Your Desk?

Why there is no single perfect timer—and how to use practical cues, short recovery pauses, and task changes throughout the workday.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

You may have heard that you should stand every 30 minutes, walk every hour, or follow a specific sit-to-stand ratio.

Those reminders can be helpful, but no single schedule fits every person, task, health condition, or workplace.

A practical goal is to avoid remaining in one position for the entire work block and to use frequent short pauses before discomfort becomes severe.

Frequent Short Breaks Are Useful

OSHA encourages short microbreaks during computer work and specifically recommends taking a five-minute break from computer tasks each hour when practical.

That break may include looking away, stretching, standing, walking, or completing another type of task.

A Break From the Screen Does Not Always Mean a Break From Work

Phone calls, filing, walking to another area, organizing supplies, or speaking with a coworker may change physical demand while work continues.

Use More Than the Clock

Time-based reminders are helpful, but your body and work process also provide useful cues.

Your Position Has Not Changed

If you have been sitting, standing, leaning, or looking in one direction for a long time, choose a different position.

Stiffness Is Beginning

Respond when the neck, shoulders, back, hips, hands, or legs first begin to feel tired or restricted.

Your Attention Is Fading

Restlessness, repeated rereading, or difficulty focusing may be a sign that a brief change of task would help.

Your Eyes Feel Tired

Look away from the screen, blink naturally, and change viewing distance.

You Are Bracing

Jaw clenching, gripping, raised shoulders, shallow breathing, or leaning forward can signal accumulated effort.

A Task Just Ended

Use natural transitions—after an email block, meeting, call, or report—as built-in opportunities to move.

Choose the Break That Fits the Time Available

Not every break needs to be long or intense.

20–30 Seconds

Change Position

Sit back, reposition the feet, relax the hands, lower the shoulders, or look away from the screen.

One Minute

Stand or Walk Briefly

Take several steps, refill water, or complete a small task away from the workstation.

Two Minutes

Complete a Full Reset

Look away, stand or shift, move the shoulders and spine, and reorganize the workstation.

Five Minutes

Leave the Computer Task

Walk, stretch comfortably, speak with a coworker, or complete a different physical task.

Longer Break

Eat, Move, and Recover

Use meal and scheduled breaks for nourishment, outdoor time, walking, rest, and a real change of environment.

A Realistic Workday Rhythm

  • Change position whenever the current one begins to feel tiring.
  • Use a brief pause between meetings, calls, or focused work blocks.
  • Aim for a five-minute break from computer tasks each hour when practical.
  • Include longer meal and recovery breaks rather than relying only on microbreaks.
  • Alternate seated, standing, and walking tasks when the work allows.
  • Pair workplace breaks with regular physical activity outside of work.

How Massage Fits Into a Desk-Break Routine

Movement breaks can reduce the buildup of stiffness, while therapeutic massage can provide more focused support for areas that remain overworked.

Neck and Shoulder Recovery

Massage may help reduce tension related to concentration, screen use, and sustained arm positioning.

Back and Hip Comfort

Therapeutic work can address areas that feel stiff or fatigued after long periods of sitting or standing.

Forearm and Hand Support

Massage can include muscles affected by typing, mouse use, gripping, and repetitive device handling.

Movement Readiness

When muscular guarding decreases, walking, stretching, and regular activity may feel easier to continue.

Body Awareness

A session may help you recognize bracing, breath-holding, leaning, or shoulder elevation earlier.

Planned Maintenance

Regular massage can support an ongoing workplace wellness routine instead of waiting until pain disrupts work.

Standing Is Not the Only Break

Standing still at the same workstation may change some muscular demands, but it does not provide the same variety as walking, changing tasks, or moving through different positions.

The goal is not to replace prolonged sitting with prolonged standing. The goal is regular variation.

Breaks Cannot Fix an Unsustainable Workload

Microbreaks are helpful, but they cannot compensate for chronic understaffing, skipped meals, excessive hours, inadequate sleep, unsafe working conditions, or a workstation that cannot be adjusted.

Workplace wellness should include both personal habits and reasonable working conditions.

When to Seek Medical Care

Persistent or worsening pain, weakness, numbness, severe swelling, loss of coordination, chest pain, shortness of breath, symptoms after an injury, or discomfort that interferes with work, sleep, or daily activity should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

You do not need to follow one perfect timer to care for your body at work.

Frequent position changes, brief recovery pauses, longer breaks, regular movement, and massage can work together.

The best schedule is one you can repeat consistently before tension becomes overwhelming.
