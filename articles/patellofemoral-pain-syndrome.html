Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome: Why the Front of the Knee Hurts

Pain around or behind the kneecap can make stairs, squatting, kneeling, running, and even prolonged sitting uncomfortable. Learn what patellofemoral pain syndrome means, why it may develop, how activity and surrounding muscles can influence symptoms, and where massage may fit within a broader recovery plan.

The quick answer

Patellofemoral pain syndrome describes pain around or behind the kneecap where the patella meets the thigh bone. Symptoms often increase when the knee is bent and loaded, such as during squatting, stairs, running, jumping, kneeling, or sitting with the knee bent for a long time. Massage may support surrounding muscle comfort, but lasting improvement commonly requires appropriate activity changes and progressive strengthening.

What is patellofemoral pain syndrome?

The kneecap, or patella, sits at the front of the knee and moves within a shallow groove at the end of the thigh bone. This relationship is known as the patellofemoral joint.

Patellofemoral pain syndrome, often shortened to PFPS, is a broad term for pain felt around or behind the patella. It is sometimes called runner’s knee, although runners are not the only people who experience it.

PFPS does not necessarily mean that the kneecap is damaged, dislocated, or permanently “out of place.” Symptoms may reflect how much load the joint is experiencing, how quickly activity has increased, and how effectively the surrounding muscles are managing that demand.

Kneecap

Patella

The patella is embedded within the quadriceps tendon and improves the leverage of the thigh muscles as the knee bends and straightens.

Thigh bone

Femoral groove

The underside of the patella moves along a groove at the end of the femur as the knee travels through its range of motion.

Support system

Muscles and tendons

The quadriceps, hips, glutes, hamstrings, calves, tendons, and nervous system all contribute to controlling the knee during movement.

What does patellofemoral pain feel like?

PFPS often develops gradually rather than after one dramatic injury. Symptoms may affect one or both knees and can vary from day to day.

  • A dull or aching pain around, beneath, or behind the kneecap.
  • Discomfort during stairs, especially when going downstairs.
  • Pain during squatting, lunging, kneeling, running, or jumping.
  • Aching after sitting with the knee bent for a prolonged period.
  • Discomfort when standing after a long car ride, movie, meeting, or work session.
  • Mild grinding, crackling, or popping sensations with knee movement.
  • Tenderness around the borders of the kneecap.
  • Reduced tolerance for repeated lower-body activity.

Noise does not automatically mean damage

Knees can click, pop, or crackle without pain or serious injury. Sound becomes more clinically important when it occurs with substantial pain, swelling, locking, instability, trauma, or loss of function.

Why certain activities aggravate the front of the knee

Patellofemoral joint demand generally increases when the knee is deeply bent while supporting body weight or producing force. This does not mean those activities are harmful. It means that symptoms may appear when the current demand exceeds the knee’s present capacity.

Stairs and hills

The quadriceps must control the body as the knee bends, particularly when descending stairs or walking downhill.

Squats and lunges

Deeper knee bending increases the work required from the thigh, hip, and knee-supporting muscles.

Running and jumping

Repeated impact and force production can expose a sudden increase in training load, fatigue, or strength limitations.

Prolonged sitting

Keeping the knee bent for a long time may increase pressure and sensitivity around the patellofemoral joint.

Kneeling

Direct pressure at the front of the knee may be uncomfortable, particularly when the area is already irritated.

Sudden activity increases

Adding mileage, hills, stairs, jumping, lifting, or sports faster than the body can adapt may provoke symptoms.

Why does patellofemoral pain develop?

PFPS rarely has one universal cause. It is often more useful to think of it as a mismatch between the demands placed on the knee and the capacity of the person’s current movement system.

1

Training or activity load

A rapid increase in running, jumping, stairs, squatting, occupational demands, or recreational activity may exceed the joint’s current tolerance.

2

Hip and thigh strength

The quadriceps and hip muscles help manage alignment, absorb force, and control the leg during stepping, landing, and changing direction.

3

Movement coordination

Fatigue or reduced control at the trunk, hip, knee, or foot may change how force is distributed during repeated movement.

4

Mobility limitations

Limited ankle, hip, or thigh mobility may influence how a person squats, walks, runs, climbs stairs, or performs work tasks.

5

Recovery and fatigue

Reduced sleep, insufficient recovery, repeated demanding activity, or returning too quickly after time away can lower exercise tolerance.

6

Individual anatomy

Bone shape, joint alignment, foot posture, prior injury, and other individual characteristics may influence symptoms, but anatomy alone does not determine pain.

Pain does not always mean the knee is wearing out

Patellofemoral pain can be persistent and frustrating, but many people improve by temporarily adjusting aggravating activities and progressively rebuilding strength and movement tolerance.

PFPS is not the same as every type of front-of-knee pain

Several conditions can cause discomfort near the kneecap. A medical professional may need to evaluate the knee when the diagnosis is unclear, symptoms are significant, or improvement does not occur.

Patellar tendinopathy

This commonly causes more localized pain in the tendon just below the kneecap, often during jumping, landing, or powerful knee extension.

Osgood-Schlatter disease

This occurs in growing adolescents and usually causes pain and prominence at the bony area below the knee where the patellar tendon attaches.

Patellar instability

The kneecap may shift excessively or dislocate, producing instability, swelling, apprehension, or a clear traumatic event.

Patellofemoral arthritis

Arthritis involves changes to the joint surfaces and is not interchangeable with patellofemoral pain syndrome.

Meniscus or ligament injury

Trauma, swelling, locking, instability, or difficulty bearing weight may indicate injury to another knee structure.

Bursitis or plica irritation

Irritated soft tissues near the front or inner knee may produce more localized tenderness, swelling, snapping, or activity-related discomfort.

Can massage help patellofemoral pain?

Massage cannot diagnose PFPS or change the structure of the patellofemoral joint. However, massage may support short-term comfort when surrounding muscles feel tense, guarded, fatigued, or overworked.

Quadriceps tension

Comfortable work to the thigh may reduce the sensation of tightness after running, stairs, squatting, lifting, or prolonged lower-body activity.

Hip and glute tension

The hips help control the thigh during stepping, landing, and changing direction. These muscles may become tired or guarded when the knee is irritated.

Calf and lower-leg fatigue

Calf tension or ankle stiffness may influence walking, squatting, and stair mechanics. Massage may support comfort in these surrounding tissues.

General relaxation

Pain can lead to bracing and whole-body tension. A comfortable session may help reduce guarding and support relaxation.

Massage works best as one part of the plan

Manual therapy may provide short-term relief, but it should generally complement activity management and an appropriate strengthening program rather than replace them.

What massage cannot do

Massage cannot confirm the diagnosis

Front-of-knee pain can come from multiple structures. A massage therapist cannot determine the diagnosis from pain location alone.

Massage cannot strengthen the knee

Massage does not replace progressive exercises that build quadriceps, hip, calf, balance, and movement capacity.

Massage cannot correct anatomy

External pressure cannot permanently reposition the kneecap, reshape the joint, or guarantee a specific tracking pattern.

Massage cannot make overload disappear

Symptoms may continue when activity demands remain greater than the knee’s current ability to tolerate them.

More pressure is not necessarily better

Aggressive pressure directly around an irritated kneecap may worsen sensitivity. Massage does not need to be painful or applied directly to the most uncomfortable location to support surrounding muscle comfort.

What commonly helps patellofemoral pain?

Recovery plans should be individualized. The right approach depends on symptoms, activity demands, strength, mobility, prior injury, and whether another diagnosis must first be ruled out.

Temporarily reduce aggravating load

This may mean adjusting running distance, hills, stairs, jumping, squat depth, kneeling, or training frequency rather than stopping every form of movement.

Keep comfortable movement in the day

Short walks, gentle knee movement, position changes, and lower-impact activity may be more tolerable than prolonged rest.

Strengthen the hips and knees

Progressive exercise may target the quadriceps, glutes, hip muscles, calves, and trunk based on the person’s needs.

Restore useful mobility

Hip, ankle, calf, and thigh mobility may be addressed when limitations affect the person’s daily or athletic movement.

Rebuild capacity gradually

Stairs, running, jumping, lifting, and sport should be increased in manageable steps rather than all at once.

Watch the overall trend

A mild short-term response does not always mean an activity is harmful, but symptoms that continually worsen may signal that the current dose is too high.

Practical self-care ideas

  • Break up prolonged sitting by standing or gently moving the knee.
  • Temporarily reduce the depth or number of painful squats, lunges, stairs, or jumps.
  • Avoid repeatedly testing the knee with the exact movement that irritates it.
  • Use comfortable movement rather than forcing a deep stretch.
  • Resume running, jumping, or demanding lower-body activity gradually.
  • Wear footwear appropriate for the activity and replace shoes that are no longer supportive or comfortable.
  • Track changes in training volume, hills, surfaces, work duties, sleep, and recovery.
  • Seek rehabilitation guidance when symptoms repeatedly return or limit important activities.

There is no single perfect knee position

Knees are designed to bend, straighten, rotate slightly, and accept load. The goal is not to avoid every movement variation. The goal is to develop enough strength and tolerance for the activities that matter to you.

When to seek medical care

Arrange prompt evaluation when symptoms are significant

Seek medical care for inability to bear weight, major swelling, visible deformity, a locked knee, repeated giving way, loss of motion, redness or heat with fever, numbness, unusual foot color or temperature, severe pain, or symptoms following a collision, fall, forceful twist, or dislocation.

Evaluation is also appropriate when pain continues to worsen, repeatedly returns, interferes with sleep or normal activity, or does not improve with reasonable activity modification.
