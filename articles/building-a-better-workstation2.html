Workplace Wellness

Building a Better Workstation

A supportive workstation does not have to be perfect or expensive. Small adjustments can make daily tasks feel more comfortable and help reduce unnecessary strain.

FlowResources • Workplace Setup Guide

Your workstation should support the way you work—not force your body to hold one rigid position all day. The best setup is one that keeps frequently used items within comfortable reach, encourages neutral positioning, and allows you to change positions regularly.

The goal is not “perfect posture.” It is a setup that reduces avoidable effort while giving your body enough variety and movement throughout the day.

Start With How You Actually Work

Before buying new equipment, pay attention to your current habits. Notice which tasks take up most of your day and where discomfort begins to appear.

A workstation used mainly for video calls may need a different arrangement than one used for writing, design work, data entry, or switching between a laptop and paper documents.

Ask yourself:

  • Which items do I use most frequently?
  • Do I regularly lean forward to see the screen?
  • Am I reaching for the mouse or keyboard?
  • Do my feet rest securely on the floor?
  • Does my neck stay turned toward a second screen?
  • Am I staying in one position for long periods?

These observations can help you identify which adjustments will make the greatest difference.

1. Adjust Your Chair First

Your chair establishes your working height. Adjust it before changing the position of your monitor, keyboard, or other equipment.

1

Place Your Feet

Keep both feet supported by the floor or a stable footrest. Avoid leaving them suspended beneath the chair.

2

Support Your Legs

Sit far enough back for the chair to support you while leaving a small amount of space behind your knees.

3

Use the Backrest

Allow the backrest to share the work of supporting your torso instead of sitting forward without support all day.

4

Relax Your Shoulders

Adjust your chair and work surface so your shoulders do not need to lift while typing or using the mouse.

No Adjustable Chair?

You can still improve support with simple modifications. A firm cushion may raise the sitting height, a small rolled towel may add lower-back support, and a sturdy box or footrest can support the feet.

Make sure any modification is stable and does not cause you to slide forward.

2. Set a Comfortable Desk Height

When using the keyboard and mouse, your upper arms should be able to remain close to your sides. Your forearms can rest approximately parallel to the floor or angle slightly downward.

If the desk is too high, you may lift your shoulders or bend your wrists upward. If it is too low, you may round forward or lower your head toward the work surface.

What You Notice Possible Adjustment
Shoulders lifting while typing Raise the chair, lower the keyboard, or use a keyboard tray.
Feet no longer reach the floor Add a stable footrest after raising the chair.
Leaning forward over the desk Move the keyboard, mouse, and frequently used items closer.
Wrists bending upward Lower the work surface or adjust keyboard placement.

The desk does not need to match a universal measurement. It needs to work with your body, chair, equipment, and primary tasks.

3. Position Your Monitor

Place your primary monitor directly in front of you whenever possible. This helps reduce the amount of time spent with your head or torso rotated to one side.

Monitor positioning basics

  • Keep the screen close enough that you can read comfortably without leaning forward.
  • Position the top portion of the screen near or slightly below eye level.
  • Tilt the screen as needed to reduce glare.
  • Increase text size instead of moving your face closer to the screen.
  • Keep your most frequently used monitor centered in front of you.

Using Two Monitors

When one monitor is used much more often than the other, center the primary monitor and place the secondary screen beside it.

When both are used equally, place them close together with the meeting point centered in front of you. Turn your chair and torso when changing screens rather than repeatedly twisting only your neck.

Working From a Laptop

A laptop places the screen and keyboard very close together, which can make it difficult to position both comfortably at the same time.

For longer work sessions, consider raising the laptop screen and using a separate keyboard and mouse. A laptop stand is useful, but a stable stack of books may also work as a temporary solution.

4. Place Your Keyboard and Mouse Within Reach

Keep your keyboard and mouse close enough that your elbows can remain near your sides. Reaching forward or outward for long periods can increase the workload on the shoulders, upper back, and neck.

Keyboard

  • Center the keyboard around the keys you use most.
  • Keep it close enough that you do not need to lean forward.
  • Let your wrists remain comfortable rather than forcing them into a sharply bent position.
  • Use light pressure when typing whenever possible.

Mouse

  • Place the mouse beside the keyboard rather than far out to the side.
  • Use the whole arm to guide larger movements instead of relying only on the wrist.
  • Adjust pointer speed if you are repeatedly lifting and repositioning the mouse.
  • Consider keyboard shortcuts to reduce repetitive mouse use.

About Wrist Rests

A wrist rest can provide support during pauses, but avoid pressing the wrist firmly into it while actively typing or using the mouse. Keep the contact light and allow your fingers and hands to move freely.

5. Arrange Frequently Used Items

Items you use throughout the day should be easy to reach without repeated twisting, stretching, or leaning.

  • Keep your phone, notebook, and other daily tools within comfortable reach.
  • Place documents on a holder near the monitor when frequently referencing them.
  • Use a headset or speaker function during longer calls instead of holding the phone between your shoulder and ear.
  • Store less frequently used supplies farther away so they do not crowd the main work area.

Not every item needs to be reachable from your chair. Occasionally standing to retrieve something can create a natural movement break.

6. Pay Attention to Lighting

Poor lighting can contribute to squinting, leaning toward the screen, or holding the head in an awkward position to avoid glare.

  • Position the screen away from direct reflections when possible.
  • Use blinds, curtains, or screen adjustments to manage window glare.
  • Add a task light for paperwork instead of relying only on the monitor.
  • Adjust screen brightness so it feels comfortable within the room.

If you continually move closer to the screen despite making adjustments, an eye examination may help identify whether vision changes are contributing to the problem.

7. Build Movement Into Your Workday

Even a carefully arranged workstation cannot replace movement. Remaining in any one position for too long may become uncomfortable, including positions commonly described as “good posture.”

Reset Let your shoulders soften and change your sitting position.
Move Stand, walk, stretch, or complete a task away from the desk.
Refocus Look away from the screen and give your eyes a different viewing distance.

Movement breaks do not always need to be long or formal. Standing during a call, walking to refill your water, changing tasks, or taking several relaxed breaths can all create useful variation.

Use Reminders Strategically

A timer, calendar notification, water bottle, or regularly scheduled task can serve as a cue to move. Choose reminders that fit naturally into your day instead of creating another source of stress.

Standing Desks Still Require Variety

A standing desk can make it easier to alternate positions, but standing all day is not automatically better than sitting all day.

When standing, keep the keyboard and mouse within easy reach, avoid locking your knees, and shift your weight periodically. A supportive mat or footrest may also provide more options for changing position.

The greatest benefit often comes from the ability to move between sitting, standing, and walking rather than treating one position as the only correct choice.

Your Workstation Check

Use this checklist after setting up your space:

  • My feet are supported by the floor or a stable footrest.
  • My chair supports me without pressing into the backs of my knees.
  • My shoulders can remain relaxed while I work.
  • My keyboard and mouse are within comfortable reach.
  • My primary screen is positioned in front of me.
  • I can read the screen without leaning forward.
  • Frequently used items are easy to reach.
  • Screen glare and room lighting feel manageable.
  • My setup allows me to change positions throughout the day.

Start With One Adjustment

You do not need to rebuild your entire workspace at once. Begin with the issue that affects you most.

That might mean raising your screen, bringing the mouse closer, adding foot support, increasing the text size, or setting a reminder to stand between tasks.

Use the updated setup for several workdays, notice how it feels, and continue adjusting as needed. Comfort can change depending on the task, the length of the workday, and what your body needs at that moment.

The FlowTherapy Takeaway

A better workstation supports comfortable work without expecting your body to remain perfectly still. Arrange your space to reduce unnecessary reaching and strain, then give yourself permission to move, reposition, and reset throughout the day.
