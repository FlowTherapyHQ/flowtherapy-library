How Often Should You Get a Massage?

How your goals, symptoms, schedule, budget, and response to care can help you build a massage routine that makes sense for you.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

“How often should I get a massage?” sounds like a question with one simple answer.

It is not.

One person may schedule massage occasionally for relaxation. Another may temporarily receive sessions more frequently while addressing a specific concern. Someone else may prefer a consistent monthly routine because it fits their lifestyle and budget.

The most appropriate schedule is not determined by a universal rule. It depends on why you are seeking massage, how your body responds, what else is happening in your care, and whether the schedule remains useful and sustainable.

The Practical Answer

Schedule massage often enough to support a clearly defined goal, but not so often that the appointments become financially, physically, or logistically difficult to maintain. Begin with a reasonable plan, observe your response, and adjust the frequency based on results rather than habit or pressure.

Start With Your Reason for Booking

Massage frequency should begin with the purpose of the session.

Are you scheduling because you want time to relax? Are you dealing with tension related to work or exercise? Are you recovering from travel? Are you receiving massage as part of a broader healthcare plan?

Different goals may call for different schedules. A plan designed for short-term focused care may not look the same as a long-term wellness routine.

Different Goals, Different Rhythms

These examples are starting points for discussion—not medical prescriptions or requirements.

Relaxation

Occasional or Routine Care

Some clients schedule when they feel the need for restorative time. Others prefer a predictable monthly or seasonal appointment as part of their self-care routine.

Recurring Tension

Observe the Pattern

A client may begin with appointments closer together, then increase the time between sessions as symptoms become easier to manage.

Active Lifestyle

Match Training and Recovery

Massage may be scheduled around demanding training periods, events, travel, or recovery needs rather than at the same frequency year-round.

Maintenance

Choose a Sustainable Routine

Some people find that regular appointments help them notice tension earlier and maintain a consistent wellness routine.

Focused Concern

Use a Defined Trial Period

A short series may be considered when there is a specific goal, followed by reassessment rather than indefinite frequent care.

Special Circumstances

Coordinate With Your Care Team

Pregnancy, surgery, injury, chronic illness, cancer treatment, medications, and rehabilitation may require medical guidance and modifications.

The right massage schedule is not the most frequent schedule. It is the one that remains useful, responsive, and sustainable.

A Massage Schedule Exists on a Spectrum

The following timeframes describe common ways people organize care. They do not guarantee a particular outcome.

As Needed

Flexible Appointments

Appropriate for clients who want occasional relaxation, post-travel care, or support during unusually demanding periods.

Every 6–8 Weeks

Periodic Maintenance

May suit clients whose symptoms remain manageable for longer periods or who prefer a lower-frequency wellness routine.

Monthly

Consistent Wellness Routine

A common option for clients who value predictable care, relaxation, and regular attention to recurring tension.

Every 2–3 Weeks

More Frequent Support

May be considered during demanding work, training, or life periods when the benefits of a session do not last a full month.

Weekly

Short-Term Focused Plan

Sometimes used for a limited period with a clear goal, appropriate tolerance, and regular reassessment.

More Than Weekly

Specific and Closely Monitored

Usually connected to a specialized setting, research protocol, athletic schedule, or coordinated healthcare plan—not routine care for every client.

Why Is There No Universal Recommendation?

Massage research does not use one standard frequency for every condition, technique, or outcome.

Studies may use different session lengths, methods, treatment schedules, participant populations, and comparison groups. Benefits found in one research protocol cannot automatically be transferred to every person or massage practice.

Some research suggests that massage may offer temporary relief for certain types of discomfort. Temporary benefit can still be meaningful, but it also means the schedule should be evaluated honestly rather than treated as a guaranteed cure.

What Should Influence Your Schedule?

Your massage routine should reflect the whole person—not only one tight area.

Your Goal

Relaxation, maintenance, focused therapeutic care, exercise recovery, and event preparation may each require a different approach.

Your Response

Consider how you feel during the session, afterward, and in the days that follow.

Duration of Benefit

Notice how long improvements in comfort, movement, or relaxation appear to last.

Your Daily Demands

Work, exercise, caregiving, travel, sleep, stress, and prolonged positioning can influence how quickly tension returns.

Your Health

Medical conditions, recent procedures, pregnancy, medications, injury, and changes in symptoms may require modifications or medical clearance.

Your Other Care

Massage may be one part of a broader plan that includes medical treatment, physical therapy, exercise, counseling, or other services.

Your Schedule

Care is less helpful when appointments create stress, exhaustion, transportation problems, or conflict with other priorities.

Your Budget

A sustainable plan should not depend on spending beyond what is comfortable or neglecting essential healthcare needs.

A Three-Phase Approach to Massage Frequency

Instead of committing immediately to an indefinite schedule, consider a plan with built-in reassessment.

1

Begin With a Clear Goal

Identify what you hope will change. Examples include moving your neck more comfortably, reducing work-related tension, or creating consistent restorative time.

2

Try a Defined Schedule

Choose a reasonable frequency for a limited period rather than assuming the same schedule should continue forever.

3

Reassess and Adjust

Continue, reduce, increase, or pause sessions based on your progress, comfort, needs, finances, and other care.

How Do You Know Whether Your Schedule Is Working?

A massage plan should be evaluated by more than whether you enjoyed the appointment.

Enjoyment and relaxation matter, but focused therapeutic care should also connect to a goal you can observe.

Signs to Continue—or Reconsider—the Plan

No single sign tells the whole story. Look for patterns over time.

The Current Plan May Be Useful When

  • You leave sessions feeling comfortable and supported.
  • The benefit lasts long enough to feel meaningful.
  • Movement or daily activity becomes easier.
  • You recover well without excessive soreness.
  • The appointments support—not disrupt—your other care.
  • The financial and time commitment remains manageable.

The Plan May Need Adjustment When

  • The benefit is consistently absent or extremely brief.
  • You regularly experience excessive soreness or bruising.
  • Symptoms worsen between or after sessions.
  • You feel pressured to book more often than you prefer.
  • Massage is replacing needed medical evaluation.
  • The cost or schedule creates significant stress.

Does More Frequent Massage Always Work Better?

No.

Some research protocols use frequent sessions and may report better short-term outcomes than less frequent care. That does not mean everyone needs multiple appointments each week.

More frequent care also means greater cost, time commitment, and tissue exposure. A client who becomes sore after every session may need less pressure, different techniques, a shorter appointment, or more recovery time—not simply another massage.

Frequency and intensity should be adjusted together.

Questions to Discuss With Your Massage Therapist

A professional recommendation should be explained—not imposed.

What goal are we working toward?

The schedule should connect to something more specific than “you are tight.”

Why are you recommending this frequency?

Your therapist should be able to explain the reasoning in clear, understandable language.

How will we know whether it is helping?

Agree on signs of progress such as comfort, movement, activity, or reduced interference with daily life.

When will we reassess?

A short-term plan should include a point when the schedule is reviewed.

What can I do between sessions?

Massage should not be the only strategy when movement, sleep, ergonomics, rehabilitation, stress management, or medical care may also matter.

What would make you refer me elsewhere?

A responsible therapist should recognize symptoms or situations that require another professional.

Your Budget Is a Valid Part of the Decision

Massage frequency should never be discussed as though finances do not matter.

A recommendation is not useful when it is unaffordable, unsustainable, or causes you to delay essential medical treatment, medication, housing, food, transportation, or other priorities.

Options may include shorter focused sessions, longer intervals, packages, memberships, alternating appointment lengths, or combining professional care with appropriate self-care.

You are allowed to ask for a plan that fits your real life.

What About Monthly Massage?

Monthly massage is popular because it is predictable, easy to remember, and often fits naturally into a wellness routine.

It may work well for clients whose benefits last several weeks and who want consistent care without more frequent appointments.

Monthly is not automatically the ideal frequency for every goal. Some clients may prefer longer intervals, while others may benefit from temporarily closer appointments followed by a maintenance schedule.

What About Massage Only When Something Hurts?

Seeking massage only during a flare-up is a valid choice, but it can make it harder to distinguish ordinary recurring tension from a problem that needs evaluation.

Some clients prefer periodic care before discomfort becomes disruptive. Others find that massage is most valuable only during demanding periods.

Neither approach is morally better. The question is whether the pattern supports your goals.

Common Massage-Frequency Myths

Myth: Everyone needs weekly massage.

Weekly care may be appropriate for a defined goal or short-term plan, but it is not a universal requirement.

Myth: Monthly massage is always maintenance.

“Maintenance” should describe a useful plan—not simply a recurring billing schedule.

Myth: If symptoms return, you need more massage.

Returning symptoms may reflect workload, sleep, stress, ergonomics, exercise, injury, or a health condition that requires another approach.

Myth: Missing an appointment erases your progress.

Your wellness does not depend on perfect attendance. Massage is one supportive tool among many.

Myth: A therapist always knows the exact schedule you need.

A therapist can make recommendations, but the plan should remain collaborative and responsive to your experience.

When Should You Reassess Your Frequency?

A schedule that worked six months ago may not match your current life.

Your Goals Change

You may shift from focused care to maintenance—or decide that massage is no longer needed for the original concern.

Your Health Changes

New diagnoses, medications, surgery, pregnancy, injury, or unexplained symptoms may require modifications or clearance.

Your Life Changes

Work, caregiving, travel, exercise, finances, and stress may all affect what schedule remains realistic.

Your Response Changes

Sessions that once felt useful may become less necessary—or may need to be adjusted in technique, duration, or pressure.

Your Other Care Changes

Physical therapy, medical treatment, exercise, or counseling may change the role massage plays in your wellness plan.

You Simply Want Something Different

Personal preference is enough reason to increase, reduce, pause, or stop routine massage.

Can Massage Become Too Frequent?

Massage is generally considered low risk when performed by a properly trained practitioner, but more is not automatically harmless or beneficial.

Sessions may be too frequent or too intense when tissues remain sore, bruised, irritated, or unable to recover between appointments.

Forceful work also requires additional caution for clients with bleeding disorders, reduced sensation, fragile bones, wounds, certain medications, recent procedures, and other health concerns.

Your therapist should adjust the schedule and techniques rather than treating tolerance as something you must build through pain.

A recommendation should never feel like pressure.

You remain in control of whether, when, and how often you receive massage. A professional plan should support informed choice—not fear, guilt, or dependence.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

At FlowTherapy, massage frequency is not determined by a sales target.

Some clients benefit from a short period of more consistent care. Others prefer monthly appointments, longer intervals, or occasional sessions when life becomes especially demanding.

The goal is not to keep you on the table as often as possible. The goal is to provide thoughtful care at a frequency that supports your comfort, goals, autonomy, and real-life circumstances.

When to Seek Medical Care

Massage frequency should not be used to manage severe, unexplained, worsening, or persistent symptoms without appropriate evaluation. Seek medical care for sudden weakness, numbness, significant swelling, fever, unexplained weight loss, loss of function, severe pain, symptoms following a major injury, chest pain, difficulty breathing, or other concerning changes.

Did You Know?

Massage research may use weekly or even more frequent sessions during a study. That schedule helps researchers test a specific protocol; it does not automatically become the correct long-term schedule for every massage client.
