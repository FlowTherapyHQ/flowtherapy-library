Why You Hold Stress in Your Jaw

How clenching, grinding, concentration, stress, sleep, and everyday habits may contribute to jaw tension—and what may help.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 7-minute read

Jaw tension often develops quietly. You may notice that your teeth are pressed together while working, driving, exercising, or concentrating. You may also wake with tired jaw muscles without realizing that you clench or grind during sleep.

Stress can contribute to jaw tension, but it is not the only possible influence. Sleep disruption, medications, dental conditions, pain, concentration habits, and repetitive jaw use may also play a role.

Understanding the pattern is more useful than assuming every jaw symptom has one simple cause.

Jaw Tension Can Be a Habit, a Stress Response, or Both

Bruxism is the clenching, grinding, or gnashing of the teeth. It can occur while awake or during sleep. Awake clenching may become more noticeable during concentration or stress, while sleep bruxism may happen without conscious awareness.

Why Does the Jaw Feel Tight?

The jaw depends on muscles that open, close, and guide the lower jaw. These muscles work during chewing, speaking, swallowing, yawning, facial expression, and clenching.

If the muscles remain active longer than necessary, they may feel tired, sore, or difficult to relax. Pressing the teeth together throughout the day can create repeated muscular effort even when you are sitting still.

Jaw discomfort can also involve the temporomandibular joints, or TMJs, located just in front of the ears. TMD is the broader term for conditions affecting the jaw joints and the muscles that control jaw movement.

Signs You May Be Clenching or Grinding

These signs can have more than one cause and do not confirm a diagnosis by themselves.

Morning Jaw Soreness

The jaw may feel tired, stiff, or achy after sleep, especially when nighttime clenching or grinding is present.

Temple or Facial Discomfort

Muscles involved in chewing extend into the temples and may contribute to facial fatigue or some headaches.

Tooth Sensitivity or Wear

Repeated grinding can damage tooth surfaces, dental work, or the tissues surrounding the teeth.

Clicking, Popping, or Limited Motion

Joint sounds are common, but painful clicking, locking, or difficulty opening deserves professional evaluation.

Neck and Shoulder Tension

Jaw tension may occur alongside neck and shoulder guarding, especially during stress or prolonged concentration.

Cheek or Tongue Marks

Some people notice biting, pressure marks, or irritation inside the mouth when clenching or grinding is frequent.

Stress Does Not Always Feel Like Anxiety

Jaw tension may appear during obvious emotional stress, but it can also develop when you are highly focused, rushed, overstimulated, frustrated, or physically bracing.

Someone may clench while answering email, lifting weights, driving through traffic, playing a video game, completing detailed work, or trying to meet a deadline.

The common feature is not necessarily feeling anxious. It may simply be increased effort and reduced awareness of what the jaw is doing.

Everyday Habits That May Increase Jaw Work

Frequent Gum Chewing

Repetitive chewing increases how long the jaw muscles remain active.

Nail, Lip, or Pen Biting

Repeated biting can add muscular demand and may irritate the teeth or surrounding tissues.

Resting the Chin on the Hand

Supporting the jaw in one position for long periods may feel comfortable temporarily but can add uneven pressure.

Holding the Phone With the Shoulder

This position may combine jaw tension with sustained neck and shoulder effort.

Very Chewy or Hard Foods

When the jaw is already irritated, prolonged chewing may make symptoms more noticeable.

Teeth Touching During Concentration

Repeated tooth contact can become an automatic daytime clenching habit.

What Does a Resting Jaw Look Like?

At rest, the lips may be gently together while the upper and lower teeth remain slightly apart. The jaw should not need to be held rigidly in position.

Some clinicians teach placing the tongue lightly near the roof of the mouth behind the upper front teeth while allowing the teeth to separate. The goal is comfortable rest—not forcing the tongue or jaw into a perfect posture.

A Jaw-Tension Check-In

Use these questions as reminders, not as a test you have to perform perfectly.

Are my teeth touching?

Let them separate slightly when you are not chewing, swallowing, or speaking.

Am I gripping with my face?

Notice the jaw, lips, brow, eyes, and tongue for unnecessary effort.

Where are my shoulders?

Jaw tension may occur alongside elevated or guarded shoulders.

Have I changed positions recently?

A brief movement break may be more useful than repeatedly stretching the jaw.

Am I chewing or biting out of habit?

Gum, nails, pens, lips, and cheeks can keep the jaw working longer than necessary.

Do I need professional evaluation?

Persistent pain, tooth damage, locking, swelling, or limited opening deserves dental or medical attention.

Common Jaw-Tension Myths

Jaw symptoms are real, but common explanations can oversimplify what is happening.

Myth
Only people with TMD clench their jaw.

Clenching and grinding can occur with or without a diagnosed temporomandibular disorder.

Fact
Awake and sleep bruxism are not the same pattern.

Daytime clenching may be influenced by awareness, stress, or concentration, while sleep bruxism occurs during sleep and is not controlled consciously.

Myth
Your teeth should remain together at rest.

A commonly taught resting position keeps the lips comfortable and the teeth slightly apart.

Fact
Jaw tension can affect more than the jaw.

The temples, face, neck, shoulders, teeth, and ears may all feel involved, although these symptoms can have other causes.

Myth
More pressure is always the fastest way to release it.

Aggressive pressure may increase guarding or irritate a sensitive area. Jaw work should remain gentle, appropriate, and responsive to the client.

Fact
Dental care may be necessary when teeth are involved.

Massage cannot repair worn teeth, broken dental work, bite changes, or other dental damage.

Can Massage Help Jaw Tension?

Massage may help some people feel more comfortable in the muscles of the jaw, face, scalp, neck, and shoulders. It may also help increase awareness of daytime clenching and unnecessary muscular effort.

Jaw work should be gentle, clearly explained, and performed with ongoing consent. You may decline facial, scalp, neck, or intraoral work at any time.

Massage does not diagnose or treat tooth damage, infection, dislocation, fracture, arthritis, sleep apnea, or another medical or dental condition.

When to See a Dentist or Healthcare Provider

Seek professional evaluation when jaw symptoms are persistent, worsening, or interfering with eating, speaking, sleeping, or daily activities.

Dental evaluation is especially important for cracked teeth, worn enamel, broken restorations, tooth sensitivity, or suspected nighttime grinding.

Seek Prompt Care For

A jaw that becomes locked open or closed; inability to open the mouth normally; severe swelling; fever; significant injury; facial weakness or numbness; difficulty swallowing or breathing; visible deformity; or sudden, severe, or rapidly worsening pain.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Jaw tension is not a personal failure and does not always mean you are consciously choosing to clench.

A useful plan may include awareness of daytime habits, appropriate dental care, support for sleep and stress, gentler jaw use, movement breaks, and massage when bodywork is appropriate.

The goal is not to force the jaw to relax. It is to create more opportunities for comfort, reduced effort, and appropriate care.
