Standing Desks: Helpful or Hype?

What sit-stand desks can realistically offer, why standing all day is not the answer, and how movement variety supports workplace comfort.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Standing desks are often marketed as a cure for back pain, poor posture, fatigue, and the health effects of sitting.

They can be useful—but the desk itself is not the treatment.

The real advantage is that a sit-stand workstation can make it easier to change positions during the day.

A Standing Desk Is a Movement Tool

A height-adjustable desk can reduce uninterrupted sitting and create more opportunities to alternate between seated and standing work.

Research suggests sit-stand workstations may modestly reduce sitting time and may reduce low-back discomfort for some workers, but results vary.

Standing Longer Is Not Automatically Better

Prolonged standing can contribute to leg fatigue, foot discomfort, swelling, muscle pain, and low-back discomfort. Variety matters more than choosing one position.

What a Standing Desk May Help With

The strongest benefit is flexibility—not perfection.

Break Up Long Sitting Periods

Raising the desk can create a natural reason to stand and change muscular demand.

Make Position Changes Easier

A desk that adjusts quickly removes some of the friction involved in changing posture.

Support Certain Tasks

Standing may feel useful for phone calls, short meetings, reviewing documents, or brief computer tasks.

Increase Body Awareness

Alternating positions can make it easier to notice leaning, locked knees, elevated shoulders, or fatigue.

Sitting Still Has a Place

Seated work can be useful for detailed keyboard tasks, concentration, stability, and conserving energy.

A supportive chair, comfortable reach, relaxed shoulders, and regular breaks may be more useful than trying to avoid sitting completely.

The Goal Is Not to “Win” at Standing

You do not need to stand for a certain percentage of every hour. Start with short, comfortable intervals and respond to your body.

A Practical Sit–Stand Rhythm

There is no universal schedule. Use task demands and comfort as your guide.

Focused Typing

Sit when seated support makes detailed keyboard and mouse work easier.

Phone Calls

Stand, shift weight, or walk when the call does not require detailed screen work.

Short Tasks

Use standing for brief email review, scheduling, or document reading.

When Fatigue Appears

Change position before discomfort becomes intense. Standing fatigue is also a signal to sit or move.

Every Hour

Whenever practical, include a short recovery pause to look away, stretch, stand, or walk.

Set Up Both Positions

A standing desk is only helpful when the standing and seated heights support the task.

Screen Height

Keep the monitor where you can read without repeatedly craning the neck or leaning toward the screen.

Keyboard and Mouse

Keep the elbows near the body and avoid reaching continuously in either position.

Feet and Shoes

Use supportive footwear and a stable surface. Shift position rather than locking the knees.

Work Surface

Confirm the desk is stable, easy to raise and lower, and large enough for your equipment.

Small Habits Matter More Than Expensive Equipment

  • Change position when concentration or comfort begins to decline.
  • Walk during natural transitions between tasks.
  • Keep frequently used items within easy reach.
  • Use a chair, stool, footrest, or supportive mat when appropriate.
  • Pair sit-stand work with regular physical activity outside the workstation.

How Massage Can Support Sit–Stand Work

Even a well-designed workstation cannot eliminate every source of muscular fatigue, repetitive strain, or stress.

Neck and Shoulder Relief

Massage may help reduce tension related to screen use, concentration, and sustained arm positioning.

Back and Hip Comfort

Therapeutic work through the back, glutes, and hips may help after long periods of sitting or standing.

Leg and Foot Recovery

Standing can fatigue the calves, feet, and lower legs. Massage may support comfort and recovery.

Movement Readiness

When muscular guarding decreases, walking, stretching, and changing positions may feel easier.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Standing desks are helpful when they make movement and position changes easier.

They become less helpful when standing turns into another position held for hours.

The best workstation supports sitting, standing, moving, recovering, and adapting throughout the day.

When to Seek Medical Care

Persistent or worsening pain, new weakness or numbness, severe swelling, difficulty walking, symptoms after an injury, or discomfort that interferes with sleep or daily function should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional.
