How Breathing Affects Muscle Tension

How breathing patterns, stress, posture, and the muscles of the chest, ribs, neck, and abdomen can influence physical tension.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Breathing is automatic, but it is not always effortless.

Stress, pain, posture, illness, exercise, concentration, and emotion can change how fast you breathe, how deeply you breathe, and which muscles contribute most.

When breathing becomes more effortful, the neck, shoulders, chest, ribs, back, and abdomen may all feel involved.

Breathing Is a Whole-Body Movement

The diaphragm is the main muscle used for breathing. The muscles between the ribs also help expand the chest, while abdominal and other muscles assist more during forceful or rapid breathing.

Breathing patterns can influence muscle effort, but they are not the only explanation for tension or pain.

Important Distinction

A breathing pattern can contribute to tension without being “wrong.” Your body may be adapting to stress, activity, illness, position, or another demand.

The Muscles That Help You Breathe

Breathing involves coordinated movement through the trunk rather than one isolated muscle doing all the work.

Breathing Team

  1. Diaphragm
  2. Intercostal muscles
  3. Abdominal muscles
  4. Neck and upper-chest muscles
Diaphragm

This dome-shaped muscle sits below the lungs. During inhalation, it contracts and moves downward, increasing space in the chest.

Intercostals

The muscles between the ribs help the rib cage expand and move during breathing.

Abdominals

These muscles can assist with forceful exhalation and become more active during rapid breathing or physical exertion.

Neck and Chest

Accessory breathing muscles may contribute more when breathing is fast, labored, restricted, or highly focused in the upper chest.

How Breathing Patterns May Affect Tension

These patterns are descriptions, not diagnoses. People naturally shift between them throughout the day.

When Breathing Feels More Effortful

  • The shoulders may rise repeatedly.
  • The neck may feel more active during each breath.
  • The upper chest may move more than the lower ribs.
  • The breath may become faster or less regular.
  • Breath-holding may occur during concentration or effort.

When Breathing Feels More Settled

  • The breath may feel slower and smoother.
  • The lower ribs and abdomen may move more easily.
  • The jaw, hands, and shoulders may use less effort.
  • Exhalation may feel less rushed.
  • The body may feel more supported by the chair or floor.

Why Stress Changes Breathing

Stress prepares the body to respond to demand. Breathing may become faster, shallower, or more irregular while attention narrows and muscles become more ready for action.

This can be useful temporarily. During ongoing stress, however, the neck, shoulders, jaw, chest, and abdomen may spend more time working than is comfortable.

Stress is not the only reason breathing changes. Exercise, asthma, lung disease, pain, pregnancy, infection, medication, posture, and many other factors can also affect breathing.

A Comfortable Breathing Practice

This practice should feel easy enough to continue. Do not force a very deep breath or follow a strict count when that feels uncomfortable.

1

Choose a Supported Position

Sit with your back supported, stand comfortably, or lie down if that position feels safe and appropriate.

2

Observe Before Changing

Notice whether you are holding your breath, rushing, sighing, or using more shoulder effort than expected.

3

Allow a Gentle Inhale

Let the breath enter without trying to fill the lungs completely. Notice movement through the lower ribs or abdomen if it occurs naturally.

4

Let the Exhale Be Unhurried

Exhale comfortably without squeezing the abdomen or pushing all the air out.

5

Return to Normal Breathing

After several breaths, stop the exercise and let breathing return to its normal rhythm.

Deeper Is Not Always Better

Taking repeated, unusually large breaths can cause lightheadedness, tingling, chest discomfort, or increased anxiety in some people.

A useful breathing practice should feel comfortable, controlled, and sustainable—not dramatic.

Common Breathing Myths

Breath practices can support relaxation, but their effects should not be overstated.

Myth
Chest breathing is always wrong.

The chest and ribs normally move during breathing. Upper-chest movement may increase with exercise, illness, stress, or other demands.

Fact
The diaphragm is the primary breathing muscle.

Efficient breathing uses coordinated movement of the diaphragm, ribs, chest, abdomen, and other supporting muscles.

Myth
You should always take the deepest breath possible.

Excessively large or rapid breaths may cause uncomfortable symptoms. Comfortable, slower breathing is usually the goal.

Fact
Breathing patterns can influence muscle effort.

The neck, shoulders, ribs, chest, back, and abdomen may feel more active when breathing becomes rapid or effortful.

Myth
Breathing exercises cure every type of tension.

Muscle tension can also relate to workload, injury, illness, sleep, movement, medication, pain, and many other factors.

Fact
Breathing exercises are supportive tools.

They may help some people manage stress or reduce unnecessary effort, but they do not replace medical evaluation or treatment.

Can Massage Help With Breathing-Related Tension?

Massage may help some people feel less tension through the neck, shoulders, chest, ribs, back, or abdomen when bodywork is appropriate.

Positioning matters. A client who feels uncomfortable lying flat may prefer elevation, side-lying, or another supported position.

Massage does not treat asthma, pneumonia, blood clots, heart conditions, lung disease, or unexplained shortness of breath.

When to Seek Medical Care

Seek prompt medical attention for sudden or severe shortness of breath, chest pain, blue or gray lips, fainting, confusion, coughing blood, new weakness, or difficulty speaking because of breathing. Persistent wheezing, breathlessness, nighttime breathing problems, or breathing that limits daily activity should also be evaluated by a healthcare professional. Stop a breathing exercise if it causes dizziness, numbness, panic, worsening shortness of breath, or other concerning symptoms.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Breathing is not another posture you have to perform perfectly.

The goal is not to force the abdomen outward, suppress chest movement, or control every breath throughout the day.

The goal is to notice when breathing feels unnecessarily effortful and create more opportunities for support, comfort, movement, and recovery.
