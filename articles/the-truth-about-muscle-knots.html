The Truth About Muscle Knots

Most people have experienced an area that feels tight, tender, or "knotted". Understanding what you may be feeling can help you make informed decisions about your care.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 6-minute read

“I have a knot in my shoulder” is one of the most common statements massage therapists hear. Although the phrase muscle knot is widely used, it is an informal description rather than a precise medical diagnosis.

People often use the term to describe an area that feels unusually firm, tight, tender, or uncomfortable. The sensation is real, but the exact reason an area feels knotted may vary from person to person.

What Is a Muscle Knot?

A “muscle knot” is commonly described as a small area within a muscle that feels tighter, firmer, or more sensitive than the surrounding tissue. Some of these areas may resemble what researchers and healthcare professionals call myofascial trigger points.

Trigger points are often described as sensitive spots within a taut band of skeletal muscle. However, researchers do not have one universally accepted method for identifying them, and the scientific understanding of these tender areas continues to develop.

Increased muscle activity, repetitive movement, physical overuse, stress, protective guarding, prolonged positioning, and changes in pain sensitivity may all contribute to how an area feels.

What People Commonly Notice

  • A small area that feels firmer or tighter than nearby tissue.
  • Tenderness when pressure is applied.
  • A dull ache or feeling of heaviness.
  • Stiffness or discomfort during movement.
  • Soreness that stays in one area or seems to spread nearby.
  • A feeling that the muscle will not completely relax.
A muscle knot describes how an area feels. It does not necessarily explain why the discomfort is happening.

What Causes Them?

There is rarely one simple cause. An uncomfortable area may develop after unfamiliar exercise, repetitive activity, long periods of sitting, physically demanding work, emotional stress, limited sleep, or an injury.

Sometimes muscles tighten to protect a sensitive joint or injured area. In other situations, the nervous system may become more responsive to pressure or movement, making an area feel painful even when there is no new tissue damage.

Daily habits can also play a role. Holding a phone between your shoulder and ear, working at a poorly arranged desk, gripping tools, carrying a heavy bag, or repeatedly performing the same motion may contribute to fatigue and discomfort.

Common Myths About Muscle Knots

  • Myth: A knot is a tangled ball of muscle fibers.
    Muscles do not usually become physically tied into knots. The term describes a sensation rather than a literal knot.
  • Myth: More pressure is always better.
    Excessive pressure can cause guarding, irritation, bruising, or increased soreness. Effective massage does not have to be extremely painful.
  • Myth: A therapist can permanently remove every knot in one session.
    Massage may improve comfort and movement, but recurring tension may also be influenced by activity, stress, sleep, injury, and other health factors.
  • Myth: Every tender area needs deep massage.
    Some conditions require medical evaluation, rest, gradual movement, or another form of care rather than intense pressure.

Can Massage Help?

Massage may help reduce the sensation of tightness, encourage relaxation, and improve short-term comfort. Some people also notice that movement feels easier after a session.

Massage is not about crushing, breaking apart, or forcing tissue to release. A therapeutic approach may include gradual pressure, broader strokes, gentle movement, stretching, positional changes, and time for the nervous system to respond.

Communication matters. Pressure should remain within a range that feels productive and tolerable to you. You can always ask for less pressure, more pressure, a different technique, or for an area to be avoided completely.

What Else May Help?

  • Change positions regularly instead of remaining still for long periods.
  • Use gentle movement to keep the area comfortable and active.
  • Gradually strengthen muscles needed for work, exercise, and daily activities.
  • Review your desk, tools, or workstation for unnecessary strain.
  • Allow enough recovery time after unfamiliar or demanding activity.
  • Pay attention to stress, sleep quality, and habits such as jaw clenching.
  • Seek professional evaluation when symptoms persist or interfere with daily life.
When to Seek Medical Care

Not every painful or firm area is a simple muscle knot. Contact a qualified healthcare provider if pain is severe, worsening, persistent, or follows a significant injury. Medical evaluation is also important if discomfort is accompanied by weakness, numbness, tingling, redness, warmth, swelling, fever, unexplained weight loss, loss of coordination, or difficulty completing normal activities.

Seek urgent medical care for chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, or other symptoms that may represent a medical emergency.

Did You Know?

How tender an area feels can change from day to day. Stress, sleep, physical activity, illness, recovery, and the sensitivity of the nervous system may all influence how your body responds to pressure.
