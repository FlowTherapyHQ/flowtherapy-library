Preventing Tech Neck

How screen height, viewing distance, device habits, movement breaks, and therapeutic massage can support neck and shoulder comfort.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

“Tech neck” is a popular name for neck, shoulder, and upper-back discomfort associated with prolonged screen use.

The device is only part of the picture. Viewing distance, vision needs, workstation setup, repetition, stress, fatigue, and time spent in one position can all contribute.

The goal is not to hold your head perfectly. The goal is to reduce unnecessary strain and create more movement variety.

Your Head Does Not Need to Stay Frozen

The neck is designed to bend, rotate, and look in different directions. Temporary forward-head positions are normal.

Discomfort is more likely when the same position is repeated or sustained for long periods without enough support or recovery.

Look at the Task, Not Just the Posture

If text is too small, the screen is too far away, glare is present, or the device sits too low, you may repeatedly lean forward to see clearly.

Common Tech-Neck Patterns

These patterns are common responses to work demands—not proof that your body is damaged.

Leaning Toward the Screen

Small text, glare, visual fatigue, or a distant monitor may pull the head and trunk forward.

Looking Down at a Phone

Long messaging, scrolling, or reading sessions can keep the neck bent for extended periods.

Elevated Shoulders

A high keyboard, reaching for the mouse, cold temperatures, stress, or concentration may increase shoulder effort.

Limited Position Changes

Even a well-supported posture may become uncomfortable when held for too long.

Jaw Clenching and Breath-Holding

Stress and focused work may add effort through the jaw, neck, chest, and shoulders.

Laptop Compromise

A laptop connects the screen and keyboard, making it difficult to optimize both at the same time during long sessions.

Start With the Screen

  • Place the monitor where text is easy to read without leaning forward.
  • Increase text size before moving your face closer to the screen.
  • Reduce glare and adjust lighting when you squint or crane to see.
  • Use a laptop stand with an external keyboard and mouse for extended laptop work.
  • Bring the phone closer to eye level for longer reading or messaging sessions.
  • Schedule an eye examination when vision changes or eyestrain persists.

A Five-Minute Tech-Neck Reset

Use gentle movement. None of these steps should cause sharp pain, dizziness, or worsening symptoms.

Minute 1

Look Away and Walk

Leave the screen, stand, and walk through the room or hallway.

Minute 2

Comfortable Head Turns

Turn the head left and right through an easy range three to five times per side.

Minute 3

Shoulder and Arm Movement

Roll the shoulders, open and close the hands, and reach forward or overhead comfortably.

Minute 4

Upper-Back Movement

Gently round and extend the upper back or complete a few supported trunk turns.

Minute 5

Reset the Workstation

Bring the screen, keyboard, mouse, and frequently used items into easier reach.

Habits That Reduce Repeated Neck Strain

Small changes repeated throughout the day matter more than one perfect stretch.

Use Microbreaks

Short, frequent recovery pauses give the eyes, muscles, and tendons a chance to change demand.

Support the Forearms

Keeping the keyboard and mouse close may reduce shoulder elevation and reaching.

Change Tasks

Alternate computer work with calls, walking, filing, meetings, or other activities when possible.

Relax Unnecessary Effort

Notice jaw clenching, gripping, breath-holding, or shoulders creeping toward the ears.

Strengthen and Move Regularly

Exercise can support the neck, shoulders, trunk, and general tolerance for daily work.

Respond Early

Change position when stiffness begins rather than waiting until discomfort becomes intense.

How Massage Can Help With Tech-Related Tension

Therapeutic massage can be a valuable part of a broader workplace wellness plan, particularly when screen use leaves the neck, shoulders, jaw, chest, upper back, or forearms feeling overworked.

Reduce Muscular Guarding

Massage may help tense neck and shoulder muscles feel less guarded and easier to move.

Address More Than the Neck

Sessions can include the upper back, chest, shoulders, jaw, forearms, and hands when those areas contribute to the pattern.

Support Movement Comfort

When tension decreases, comfortable head turns, shoulder movement, walking, and exercise may feel easier.

Encourage Recovery and Awareness

Massage creates time away from the screen and may help you notice where you habitually brace or overwork.

What Massage and Ergonomics Cannot Do Alone

Massage cannot correct vision problems, diagnose nerve symptoms, repair an injury, or replace appropriate exercise, medical care, or workstation changes.

Ergonomics also cannot eliminate every discomfort when workload, stress, sleep, health conditions, or limited recovery are contributing.

When to Seek Medical Care

Seek professional evaluation for persistent or worsening pain, new weakness or numbness, loss of hand coordination, severe headaches, dizziness, symptoms after an injury, pain with fever, or discomfort that interferes with sleep, work, or normal daily activity.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Your neck is not fragile, and ordinary screen use does not require fear.

Comfort improves when the screen supports your vision, the workstation supports the task, and the day includes enough movement and recovery.

Massage can help reduce tension and make those healthier habits easier to continue.
