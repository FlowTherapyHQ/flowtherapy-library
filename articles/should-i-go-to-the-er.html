Should I Go to the ER?

Understanding stress, pain, and when to seek care

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 5-minute read

Not every ache, pain, or stressful moment is an emergency—but knowing the difference matters.

This article explores when emergency medical care may be appropriate, how chronic stress can affect both the mind and body, and why therapeutic massage can be part of a proactive wellness routine.

There are moments when seeking emergency medical care is absolutely the right decision. Chest pain, difficulty breathing, sudden weakness, severe injuries, or signs of stroke should never be ignored.

But many people also experience symptoms that feel alarming without necessarily being emergencies. Muscle tension, headaches, back pain, neck pain, dizziness, racing thoughts, chest tightness, and overwhelming anxiety can all feel frightening. Chronic stress may make those symptoms feel even more intense.

Learning when to seek emergency care, when to contact your healthcare provider, and how to manage everyday stress can help you make informed decisions while caring for your body.

When You Should Go to the Emergency Room

Seek immediate medical attention or call 911 if you experience symptoms such as:

  • Chest pain, pressure, or discomfort that could indicate a heart attack
  • Difficulty breathing or severe shortness of breath
  • Sudden numbness or weakness, especially on one side of the body
  • Difficulty speaking or understanding speech
  • Sudden confusion or loss of consciousness
  • Severe bleeding
  • Major injuries or possible broken bones
  • Seizures
  • Severe allergic reactions
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or others, or a mental health crisis involving immediate danger
When in doubt, seek medical evaluation.

Massage therapy should never delay emergency care or evaluation for serious or unfamiliar symptoms.

Stress Can Feel Like an Emergency

Stress and anxiety do not only affect the mind. They can influence the entire body.

Stress may contribute to:

  • Muscle tension
  • Neck and shoulder discomfort
  • Tension headaches
  • Jaw pain or clenching
  • Trouble sleeping
  • Digestive discomfort
  • Rapid heartbeat
  • Chest tightness
  • Fatigue
  • Difficulty concentrating

Sometimes these symptoms feel frightening enough that a person seeks emergency care to rule out something more serious. Emergency evaluation is appropriate when symptoms are severe, sudden, unfamiliar, or accompanied by warning signs.

Massage Therapy as Proactive Care

Massage therapy is not a replacement for emergency, medical, or mental health care. It should never be used to diagnose a condition or delay necessary treatment.

However, therapeutic massage may help many people:

  • Reduce perceived stress
  • Ease short-term anxiety
  • Relax tense muscles
  • Improve sleep quality
  • Support relaxation
  • Reduce some types of muscle-related discomfort
  • Support overall well-being

Think of massage as one part of a larger wellness plan, alongside exercise, sleep, nutrition, movement, medical care, and mental health support when needed.

A Different Way to Think About Wellness

Many of us wait until our bodies demand attention.

We push through headaches, ignore stiff shoulders, work through back pain, and tell ourselves we will slow down next week.

Eventually, the body gets louder.

Massage therapy cannot prevent every illness or emergency. Regularly caring for your body, however, may help you manage the physical effects of stress and notice concerns before they become more disruptive.

Wellness is not about perfection. It is about consistency.

When Massage May Be Helpful

Massage therapy may be appropriate for:

  • Stress-related muscle tension
  • Desk or computer-related discomfort
  • Muscle-related tension headaches
  • Exercise recovery
  • Difficulty relaxing
  • General aches and stiffness
  • Ongoing wellness maintenance

Seek medical evaluation first when symptoms are severe, worsening, sudden, unexplained, or accompanied by chest pain, difficulty breathing, fever, sudden weakness, neurological symptoms, or a significant injury.

Did You Know?

Stress does not only affect your mood. It can also contribute to muscle tension, headaches, digestive discomfort, disrupted sleep, and chronic pain.

Massage therapy is not a replacement for medical or mental health care, but it may help many people reduce stress, relax tense muscles, ease short-term anxiety, and support overall well-being.

Final Thoughts

Your body is remarkably good at communicating when it needs attention.

Sometimes that means calling 911.

Sometimes it means scheduling an appointment with your healthcare provider.

Sometimes it means taking a walk, improving your sleep, practicing slow breathing, or scheduling a therapeutic massage.

Listening to your body before small concerns become larger ones is one of the most meaningful investments you can make in your long-term health.
