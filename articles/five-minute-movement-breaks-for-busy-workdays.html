Five-Minute Movement Breaks for Busy Workdays

Simple movements you can use throughout your workday to interrupt prolonged sitting, reduce stiffness, and support greater comfort.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 7-minute read

If you work at a computer for much of the day, you may recognize the feeling of standing up after several hours and suddenly noticing stiffness in your neck, shoulders, back, or hips.

Supporting your body does not always require an hour at the gym. Brief movement breaks performed throughout the day can help interrupt prolonged sitting and give your muscles and joints an opportunity to change positions.

Why Movement Breaks Matter

Your body is designed to move between different positions. Muscles naturally alternate between activity and rest as you walk, reach, bend, stand, and shift your weight.

During long periods of desk work, many muscles continue working quietly in the background. The muscles of your neck and upper back support your head, your shoulders stabilize your arms, and your forearms and hands perform repeated movements as you type and use a mouse.

Even a comfortable chair or supportive workstation may begin to feel uncomfortable if you remain in the same position for several hours. Short movement breaks help create variety by changing which muscles are active and how your body is supported.

Small movements performed consistently often matter more than perfect workouts performed occasionally.

You Do Not Need a Gym

Many people assume movement only counts when it happens during a formal workout. Your body does not make that distinction.

Standing during a phone call, walking to refill your water bottle, gently moving your shoulders, or taking a lap around your home or office all count as movement.

These brief interruptions may not replace regular exercise, but they can reduce the amount of uninterrupted time you spend sitting or repeating the same task.

Consistency matters more than intensity. A routine that takes only a few minutes is often easier to repeat than a longer routine that feels difficult to fit into a busy day.

FlowBreak Featuring The Flow Five

Rather than waiting until your body feels stiff or exhausted, try taking a FlowBreak every hour or two.

The Flow Five is a gentle five-part routine designed to help you change positions and move areas that often feel stiff or tense. Complete each movement slowly and remain within a comfortable range.

  1. Chin Tuck

    Keep your eyes level and gently draw your chin straight backward. Avoid forcing your chin downward or holding your breath.

  2. Shoulder Rolls

    Slowly roll your shoulders backward several times. Allow them to relax away from your ears as you move.

  3. Chest Opener

    Gently draw your shoulders back and allow your chest to open. Avoid aggressively squeezing your shoulder blades together.

  4. Thoracic Rotation

    While seated or standing, slowly rotate your upper body to one side and then the other. Keep your movement gentle and avoid forcing the stretch.

  5. Walk and Breathe

    Stand and walk for one or two minutes. Take several slow, comfortable breaths before returning to your workspace.

FlowBreak is intended as a brief movement routine for general comfort. Stop any movement that causes pain, dizziness, numbness, or other concerning symptoms.

Make FlowBreak Part of Your Day

The easiest movement routine is the one you remember to do. Rather than relying on motivation, connect your FlowBreak to activities that already happen during your day.

Consider taking a FlowBreak:

  • After a virtual meeting
  • When you refill your water
  • After returning from the restroom
  • Before checking your next round of email
  • When your watch or phone reminds you to stand
  • Between clients, calls, or major work tasks
  • Whenever you notice yourself becoming stiff or tense

Attaching movement to an existing habit can make it feel like a normal part of the workday rather than one more task competing for your attention.

Breathing Matters Too

Many people unintentionally hold their breath or begin breathing more shallowly during periods of concentration.

Stressful deadlines, difficult conversations, and demanding projects may also lead you to clench your jaw, tighten your hands, grip the mouse, or raise your shoulders without realizing it.

A slow breath does not solve every source of tension, but it can provide a useful moment to check in with your body. As you breathe, notice whether you can soften your jaw, relax your hands, and allow your shoulders to move away from your ears.

Movement Breaks Do Not Have to Be Perfect

Missing a planned break does not mean the day is lost. You do not need to complete every movement at an exact time or perform the routine perfectly for it to be useful.

On especially busy days, your FlowBreak might be one shoulder roll, one deep breath, and a walk to another room. On other days, you may have time for the full Flow Five.

The goal is not perfection. The goal is to give your body more opportunities to move than it would have had otherwise.

How Massage Complements Movement

Movement and therapeutic massage are not competing approaches. They can complement one another.

Regular movement helps reduce the amount of time your body spends in one position. Therapeutic massage may help reduce the sensation of muscle tightness, encourage relaxation, improve comfort, and support easier movement.

For clients who spend long hours at a computer, a massage session may address more than the neck and shoulders. Depending on the client’s needs and consent, treatment may also include the upper back, chest, arms, forearms, wrists, and hands.

Massage cannot replace regular movement or correct an unsuitable workstation. It is best viewed as one part of a broader approach that may include exercise, adequate sleep, stress management, and practical changes to your daily routine.

When Movement Is Not Enough

Movement breaks may be appropriate for general stiffness related to prolonged sitting. However, persistent or worsening pain, numbness, tingling, progressive weakness, reduced coordination, loss of grip strength, or symptoms following an injury should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare provider.

Stop the routine if a movement causes sharp pain, dizziness, shortness of breath, or other concerning symptoms. Exercise and massage should not be used to delay appropriate medical care.

Did You Know?

Short periods of standing and walking can add up throughout the day. A two-minute walk completed five times creates ten additional minutes of movement without requiring a separate workout.
