Understanding Osgood-Schlatter Disease: What Active Teens and Parents Should Know

Pain and swelling at the bony bump below the kneecap commonly affect active adolescents during periods of rapid growth. Learn why Osgood-Schlatter disease develops, which activities may aggravate it, what usually supports recovery, when medical evaluation is needed, and how massage may be safely adapted.

FlowNotes Adolescent knee wellness Approximately 10-minute read

The quick answer

Osgood-Schlatter disease is irritation at the bony growth area below the kneecap where the patellar tendon attaches to the shinbone. It commonly develops in active, growing adolescents and may hurt during running, jumping, squatting, stairs, kneeling, or sports. It is usually managed by adjusting painful activity and gradually restoring strength and flexibility. Massage should avoid direct pressure over the painful bump.

What is Osgood-Schlatter disease?

Osgood-Schlatter disease is a common cause of pain at the front of the knee in children and adolescents who are still growing. Despite its name, it is not an infection or a contagious disease.

Symptoms occur at the tibial tubercle, the bony prominence near the top of the shinbone just below the kneecap. This is where the patellar tendon attaches.

During running, jumping, kicking, squatting, and other lower-body activities, the quadriceps pull through the kneecap and patellar tendon. In a growing adolescent, repeated traction at the tendon attachment can irritate the developing bone and growth area beneath it.

Front thigh

Quadriceps

The quadriceps straighten the knee and help control the body during running, landing, stairs, squatting, and kicking.

Below kneecap

Patellar tendon

The patellar tendon connects the kneecap to the upper shinbone and transfers force from the quadriceps.

Upper shinbone

Tibial tubercle

The tibial tubercle is the bony attachment point that can become painful, tender, swollen, or more prominent.

The painful area is below the knee joint

Osgood-Schlatter symptoms are typically centered over the tibial tubercle rather than deep inside the knee. Pain in another location may have a different cause and should not automatically be labeled Osgood-Schlatter disease.

Who commonly develops it?

Osgood-Schlatter disease occurs while the skeleton is still developing. It is especially common during growth spurts, when bones may lengthen faster than muscles and tendons adapt to the change.

  • Children and adolescents who are still growing.
  • Young people experiencing a rapid increase in height.
  • Athletes who run, jump, kick, squat, or change direction repeatedly.
  • Participants in soccer, basketball, volleyball, gymnastics, track, dance, football, martial arts, or similar activities.
  • Young people who recently increased training frequency, duration, or intensity.
  • Adolescents balancing school sports, club teams, private training, and recreational activity with limited recovery.

Symptoms can affect one knee or both knees. One side may be noticeably more painful than the other.

Common symptoms

Symptoms often develop gradually and may come and go depending on growth, training load, sports seasons, and recovery time.

  • Pain directly over the bony bump below the kneecap.
  • Tenderness when the tibial tubercle is touched or pressed.
  • Swelling or prominence at the tendon attachment.
  • Pain during or after running, jumping, kicking, or sprinting.
  • Discomfort with squatting, lunging, kneeling, or stairs.
  • Tightness or fatigue in the quadriceps or surrounding leg muscles.
  • Symptoms that improve with rest and return when activity increases.
  • Sensitivity when kneeling on a hard surface or when sports equipment presses against the area.

A permanent bump may remain

The tibial tubercle may remain more prominent even after pain has resolved and growth is complete. A visible bump does not necessarily mean that the area is still actively irritated.

Why certain activities aggravate it

Activities that repeatedly contract the quadriceps or place the knee in a bent, loaded position increase pulling through the patellar tendon at its attachment below the knee.

Running and sprinting

Repeated acceleration, deceleration, and impact increase the work performed by the quadriceps and patellar tendon.

Jumping and landing

Powerful takeoff and controlled landing repeatedly load the knee-extensor system.

Squats and lunges

Bending and straightening the knee under body weight or resistance can increase symptoms when the area is irritated.

Kicking

Forceful knee extension during soccer, football, martial arts, or dance can increase traction through the tendon.

Stairs and hills

The quadriceps control the body as the knee bends, particularly during stair descent or downhill movement.

Kneeling

Direct contact over the tender tibial tubercle may be uncomfortable even when the movement itself is not demanding.

The relationship between growth and activity load

Osgood-Schlatter disease does not mean that exercise is bad for a growing body. Physical activity is important for strength, coordination, confidence, bone health, and overall wellness.

Symptoms are more likely when the developing attachment point is repeatedly exposed to more force than it can currently tolerate. That balance can change during a rapid growth spurt, a busy sports season, or a sudden training increase.

Activity does not always need to stop completely

Many young people can continue some activity when symptoms are mild, movement is not significantly altered, and the activity does not cause a substantial or lasting flare.

Pushing through worsening pain is not the goal

Repeated limping, declining performance, increasing pain, or prolonged post-activity symptoms suggest that the current activity dose may be too high.

Symptoms usually improve with skeletal maturity

The condition is generally self-limited because the growth area eventually matures and becomes less vulnerable to repetitive traction.

“Growing pains” should not replace evaluation

Significant swelling, severe pain, trauma, inability to bear weight, night pain, fever, or symptoms in an unusual location need appropriate medical assessment.

Conditions that may feel similar

Not every pain below or around the kneecap is Osgood-Schlatter disease. Evaluation may be needed when the location, age, history, or symptoms do not fit the expected pattern.

Patellofemoral pain syndrome

This more commonly causes pain around or behind the kneecap, rather than directly over the tibial tubercle.

Patellar tendinopathy

This involves the patellar tendon itself and may cause more localized tendon pain during jumping or powerful knee extension.

Sinding-Larsen-Johansson syndrome

This growth-related condition causes pain near the lower edge of the kneecap rather than at the upper shinbone.

Acute fracture or avulsion injury

Sudden severe pain after forceful movement or trauma may indicate an acute injury rather than gradual overuse.

Ligament or meniscus injury

Twisting trauma, rapid swelling, locking, or instability may suggest injury to another structure within the knee.

Infection or inflammatory condition

Significant redness, heat, fever, illness, or unexplained swelling requires medical evaluation.

How is it evaluated?

A healthcare professional usually begins with the young person’s history, symptom location, activity pattern, growth stage, and a physical examination of the knee.

The clinician may assess tenderness over the tibial tubercle, swelling, walking, knee motion, strength, flexibility, and whether another condition may be causing the symptoms.

Imaging is not always necessary when the history and examination are typical. An X-ray or other testing may be considered when symptoms are severe, traumatic, unusual, persistent, or inconsistent with Osgood-Schlatter disease.

Massage cannot confirm the diagnosis

A massage therapist cannot determine whether pain is coming from a growth plate, tendon, fracture, ligament, meniscus, infection, or another condition. Young people with undiagnosed knee pain should receive appropriate evaluation.

What commonly supports recovery?

Management should reflect symptom severity, growth stage, sports demands, daily activity, and medical guidance. The aim is to keep the adolescent active while reducing repeated irritation.

Adjust the most aggravating activities

Temporarily reduce jumping, sprinting, hills, deep squats, kneeling, or sports volume when those activities repeatedly provoke symptoms.

Use relative rest rather than total shutdown

Lower-impact or less painful activity may be maintained when it does not cause limping or a meaningful worsening of symptoms.

Restore comfortable flexibility

The quadriceps, hamstrings, calves, and hips may be addressed through gentle, age-appropriate mobility when recommended.

Build lower-body strength

Progressive strengthening of the thighs, hips, glutes, calves, and trunk can improve the body’s ability to manage running, landing, stairs, and sports.

Increase training gradually

Practice volume, competition, conditioning, and private training should be coordinated so the total weekly load does not rise too quickly.

Allow adequate recovery

Sleep, nutrition, rest days, and time between demanding sessions support adaptation during growth.

Symptoms—not toughness—should guide the activity dose

Young athletes may minimize pain because they do not want to lose playing time. Parents, coaches, and clinicians should pay attention to limping, altered movement, reduced performance, and worsening symptoms after activity.

Can massage help Osgood-Schlatter symptoms?

Massage cannot change the growth plate, remove the bony prominence, or stop the pulling force at the tendon attachment. It may provide supportive comfort for surrounding muscles when the young person has been medically evaluated and massage is appropriate.

Quadriceps tension

Comfortable thigh work may help reduce the sensation of muscular tightness after running, jumping, kicking, or repeated training.

Hamstring and calf fatigue

The back of the thigh and lower leg may feel tired or tight during growth spurts or busy sports seasons.

Hip and glute tension

The hips and glutes help control the leg during running, landing, stairs, squatting, and changing direction.

General recovery and relaxation

A comfortable session may support relaxation and body awareness after demanding activity without treating the growth area itself.

Direct pressure over the bump is unnecessary

Massage can address the thigh, hips, glutes, hamstrings, calves, feet, and other surrounding areas while completely avoiding the tender tibial tubercle.

What massage cannot do

Massage cannot diagnose the condition

The therapist cannot determine whether the symptoms come from Osgood-Schlatter disease or another knee condition.

Massage cannot change the growth plate

External pressure cannot alter bone development or accelerate skeletal maturity.

Massage cannot remove the bump

A prominent tibial tubercle is part of the underlying structure and should not be pressed, manipulated, or “broken down.”

Massage cannot replace load management

Symptoms may persist when the athlete continues more running, jumping, kneeling, or competition than the area can tolerate.

Avoid deep pressure over the painful attachment

Forceful pressure, friction, scraping, percussion, aggressive stretching, or massage-gun use over the tibial tubercle may increase irritation. Pain should not be treated as proof that the technique is effective.

When massage should wait

Massage around the affected knee should be postponed when the symptoms have not been evaluated or when there are signs that a more serious condition may be present.

  • Severe pain began suddenly during a jump, sprint, kick, or collision.
  • The young person cannot bear weight or walk normally.
  • There is rapid swelling, visible deformity, or extensive bruising.
  • The knee locks, gives way, or cannot fully bend or straighten.
  • The area is red, unusually hot, draining, or accompanied by fever.
  • Pain regularly wakes the child or occurs without activity.
  • Symptoms are worsening, spreading, or affecting normal daily function.
  • A fracture, avulsion injury, infection, or another diagnosis has not been ruled out.

Guidance for parents and young athletes

For parents and caregivers

Take repeated pain seriously without treating the young person as fragile. Watch for limping, movement changes, reduced enthusiasm, sleep disruption, or symptoms lasting well beyond activity.

Coordinate sports, school athletics, conditioning, and private training so the total workload is visible rather than managed as separate activities.

For young athletes

Reporting pain early does not mean you are weak or quitting. Early activity adjustments may help you remain involved rather than waiting until symptoms become difficult to manage.

Let an adult know when pain changes how you run, jump, walk, sleep, practice, or participate in normal activities.

Practical comfort and activity ideas

  • Avoid kneeling directly on the painful bump or use comfortable padding when kneeling cannot be avoided.
  • Temporarily reduce the number of painful jumps, sprints, hills, stairs, kicks, or deep squats.
  • Use gentle thigh, hip, hamstring, and calf movement rather than forcing a painful stretch.
  • Alternate demanding training with lower-impact or recovery activities.
  • Avoid repeatedly pressing the bump to check whether it still hurts.
  • Discuss symptom-based modifications with coaches and physical education teachers.
  • Return to full practice volume gradually after symptoms settle.
  • Follow medical or rehabilitation guidance when exercises, bracing, taping, or other support has been recommended.

Comfort measures should not hide worsening symptoms

Ice, padding, massage, or over-the-counter pain relief may make activity feel easier temporarily, but symptom reduction does not automatically mean the growth area is ready for unlimited training.

When to seek medical care

Arrange an evaluation when symptoms affect function

Seek medical care when pain causes limping, prevents normal walking or sports participation, continues to worsen, does not improve with reasonable activity modification, or occurs with significant swelling, locking, instability, fever, redness, heat, night pain, numbness, or a traumatic injury.

Prompt evaluation is especially important after sudden severe pain, a forceful jump or kick, direct trauma, visible deformity, or inability to bear weight.

How FlowTherapy may support a young athlete

With parent or guardian involvement, appropriate medical evaluation, and informed consent, FlowTherapy may provide carefully modified massage for surrounding muscle tension.

A session may address the quadriceps, hamstrings, hips, glutes, calves, feet, or back while avoiding direct pressure over the painful tibial tubercle.

Positioning can be adapted so the adolescent is not required to kneel, deeply bend the knee, or maintain an uncomfortable position.

Relevant services may include a carefully modified Focused Flow or Lower Body Flow. Any stretching or movement should remain comfortable, age-appropriate, and consistent with medical or rehabilitation guidance.
