What to Expect During Your First Massage

Knowing what happens before, during, and after a massage can help you feel more prepared, comfortable, and confident throughout your first session.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 7-minute read

Trying massage therapy for the first time can feel exciting, but it may also bring questions or uncertainty. You may wonder what to wear, how much clothing to remove, whether massage will hurt, or what you are expected to do during the session.

A professional massage should be collaborative, respectful, and centered around your comfort, goals and informed choice. You are not expected to know everything ahead of time. Your massage therapist should explain the process, answer your questions, and give you meaningful choices throughout the experience.

Before Your Appointment

You may be asked to complete an intake form before your first session. This form helps your massage therapist understand your health history, current concerns, medications, injuries, allergies, surgeries, and goals for the appointment.

Share information that may affect your care, including recent injuries, pregnancy, skin conditions, blood-clotting concerns, numbness, unexplained pain, or medical restrictions. This information allows your therapist to modify the session or recommend medical clearance when appropriate.

You do not need to arrive with a perfect explanation of what is bothering you. Describing where you feel discomfort, when you notice it, and what activities make it better or worse can provide a helpful starting point.

Your Initial Conversation

Before the massage begins, your therapist should talk with you about your goals and preferences. You may discuss areas of tension, desired pressure, past massage experiences, and any areas you do not want addressed.

This is also the time to ask questions. Your therapist may explain the techniques they are considering, how you will be positioned, what areas may be included, and how draping will be handled.

Consent is an ongoing conversation, not a one-time question. Agreeing to massage does not mean agreeing to every technique or every area of the body.

What Should You Wear?

Wear comfortable clothing to your appointment. Depending on the type of massage, you may remain fully clothed or be invited to undress to your personal comfort level.

You are never required to remove all of your clothing. You may keep on any clothing that helps you feel secure and comfortable. Your therapist can adjust techniques based on what you choose to wear.

When undressing is part of the session, your therapist should leave the room and give you privacy. You will usually position yourself beneath a sheet or blanket before the therapist returns.

Understanding Draping and Privacy

Professional draping means that your body remains covered except for the specific area being massaged. For example, when one leg is being addressed, the rest of your body remains covered.

Draping should feel secure and respectful. Breast tissue and genital areas are not exposed or massaged during a standard therapeutic massage. Gluteal work, when appropriate and agreed upon, may be performed through the sheet or with professional draping based on applicable laws, training, and your consent.

You may decline work on any area without giving a reason. Your boundaries should be respected without pressure or judgment.

Your comfort is not an interruption to the massage. It is an essential part of the session.

What Happens During the Massage?

Your therapist may begin with lighter pressure to warm the tissues and understand how your body responds. Techniques may then be adjusted based on your goals, comfort, and feedback.

You may be asked to change positions during the session. Your therapist should explain what is needed and provide privacy while you reposition beneath the draping.

You do not need to make conversation. Some clients enjoy talking, while others prefer quiet. Either choice is appropriate. You may also close your eyes, breathe naturally, or fall asleep.

Massage may create sensations of pressure, tenderness, warmth, stretching, or relaxation. Therapeutic work can sometimes feel intense, but it should not require you to endure sharp, burning, alarming, or overwhelming pain.

It Is Okay To...

  • Ask questions before or during the session.
  • Request more or less pressure.
  • Decline massage on any area of your body.
  • Change your mind after initially giving consent.
  • Ask for a technique to stop or be modified.
  • Request another blanket or a temperature adjustment.
  • Ask to reposition yourself or take a short break.
  • Use the restroom during the appointment.
  • Choose conversation, quiet, or a combination of both.
  • End the session if you feel uncomfortable or unsafe.

How Much Pressure Should Massage Use?

The right amount of pressure varies from person to person and may change during the same session. Deeper pressure is not automatically more therapeutic, and pain is not proof that a technique is working.

Productive pressure may feel focused or intense while still allowing you to breathe comfortably and avoid bracing. If you begin holding your breath, tightening your muscles, pulling away, or feeling overwhelmed, the pressure may need to be adjusted.

Your therapist cannot always tell how a technique feels to you. Honest feedback helps create a safer and more effective session.

After the Massage

When the hands-on portion ends, your therapist should leave the room so you can get dressed privately. Take your time when sitting up, especially if you feel deeply relaxed or slightly lightheaded.

Your therapist may check in about how you feel and offer general aftercare suggestions. These may include gentle movement, returning gradually to normal activity, drinking according to your usual needs, or monitoring how your body responds.

Some people feel relaxed immediately, while others feel energized, tired, emotionally reflective, or mildly sore. Responses vary, and there is no single “correct” way to feel after massage.

Tips for a Comfortable First Session

  • Arrive with enough time to avoid feeling rushed.
  • Complete your intake information honestly and thoroughly.
  • Share your goals, concerns, and boundaries before the massage begins.
  • Speak up when something feels uncomfortable or unclear.
  • Avoid comparing your preferences with someone else’s massage experience.
  • Remember that you remain in control of your participation throughout the session.
When to Postpone Massage or Seek Medical Guidance

Contact your massage therapist before the appointment if you have a fever, contagious illness, active vomiting or diarrhea, a new rash, a suspected blood clot, a recent significant injury, or a medical procedure with activity restrictions.

Seek guidance from a qualified healthcare provider when you have unexplained or worsening symptoms, severe pain, significant swelling, new weakness, numbness, breathing difficulty, chest pain, or another concern that may require medical evaluation. Massage should not delay urgent or necessary medical care.

Did You Know?

You do not have to wait until something feels wrong to communicate. Sharing preferences early—such as pressure, room temperature, positioning, music, or areas to avoid—can help your therapist personalize the session from the beginning.
