Why Stress Causes Muscle Tension

Stress does not only affect your thoughts and emotions. It can also change how your body breathes, moves, rests, and holds tension.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 5-minute read

Have you ever noticed your shoulders creeping toward your ears during a difficult day? Maybe your jaw feels tight, your neck becomes stiff, or your lower back begins to ache when you feel overwhelmed. These physical reactions are common because stress affects both the mind and the body.

Muscle tension is part of the body’s natural response to a challenge or perceived threat. This response can be useful for short periods, but ongoing stress may leave some muscles working harder and resting less than they normally would.

Your Body’s Stress Response

When your brain senses pressure, uncertainty, or danger, it prepares your body to respond. Your heart rate and breathing may increase, your attention becomes sharper, and certain muscles tighten as the body gets ready for action.

This reaction is often called the fight-or-flight response. It developed as a protective system, helping people respond quickly when safety or survival was at risk. Today, the same response may be activated by deadlines, financial worries, relationship concerns, difficult conversations, caregiving responsibilities, or other everyday pressures.

The body does not always distinguish between an immediate physical threat and a stressful situation that exists mainly in your thoughts. Even when you are sitting still, your nervous system may respond as though you need to prepare, protect yourself, or remain on alert.

Where Stress-Related Tension Often Appears

  • The jaw, especially when clenching or grinding the teeth.
  • The neck and shoulders, particularly during desk work or emotional strain.
  • The scalp and forehead, sometimes contributing to tension-type headaches.
  • The upper and lower back, especially when stress affects posture or breathing.
  • The hips and pelvic area, where some people unconsciously brace or tighten.
Stress may begin as an emotional experience, but the body often carries part of the conversation.

Why the Tension Can Continue

Ideally, the body relaxes after a stressful event has passed. However, repeated or ongoing stress may make it difficult to fully return to a relaxed state. You may continue holding your breath, raising your shoulders, clenching your jaw, or bracing your abdomen without realizing it.

These patterns may become more noticeable when combined with long periods of sitting, repetitive movement, limited sleep, dehydration, physical overuse, or an existing injury. Stress may not be the only cause of discomfort, but it can influence how strongly discomfort is experienced and how difficult it feels to relax.

Tension can also create a cycle. Stress contributes to muscle tightness, tightness contributes to discomfort, and discomfort can create additional stress. Interrupting that cycle may require both physical care and strategies that help the nervous system feel safer and more settled.

Ways to Help Your Body Release Tension

  • Pause periodically and notice whether you are clenching your jaw or lifting your shoulders.
  • Take several slow, comfortable breaths without forcing yourself to breathe deeply.
  • Change positions throughout the day instead of remaining in one posture for hours.
  • Use gentle movement, walking, or stretching to reduce physical bracing.
  • Create a calming transition between work, responsibilities, and rest.
  • Prioritize consistent sleep and recovery whenever possible.
  • Consider massage as one part of a broader stress-management and wellness routine.

How Massage May Help

Massage may help reduce the feeling of muscular tightness, encourage comfortable movement, and provide an opportunity to slow down and reconnect with the body. A quieter environment, supportive touch, and unhurried breathing may also help some people shift away from a constant state of alertness.

Massage does not remove the source of stress, and it is not a replacement for medical care or mental health support. However, it can be a helpful part of a well-rounded approach that may also include movement, rest, counseling, medical care, mindfulness practices, or changes to daily routines.

Trauma-informed massage places particular importance on communication, consent, comfort, and choice. You should always feel able to ask questions, request changes, decline an area of work, or end a session.

When to Seek Medical Care

Stress is not the only possible cause of muscle pain or tightness. Contact a qualified healthcare provider if discomfort is severe, persistent, worsening, follows an injury, interrupts sleep, or is accompanied by weakness, numbness, tingling, fever, swelling, unexplained weight loss, chest pain, shortness of breath, or other concerning symptoms. Call emergency services for symptoms that may represent a medical emergency.

Did You Know?

You may hold tension without noticing it. A brief body check-in can help: soften your jaw, lower your shoulders, relax your hands, and notice whether you are holding your breath.
