The 2-Minute Reset Between Meetings

A practical transition that helps the eyes, neck, shoulders, back, hips, and nervous system recover before the next demand begins.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 7-minute read

Back-to-back meetings can turn an entire workday into one long period of sitting, screen use, concentration, and muscular bracing.

When one meeting ends and the next begins immediately, the body receives no clear transition between tasks.

Two minutes will not replace a full break—but it can interrupt stiffness, release unnecessary effort, and help you begin the next meeting with more awareness.

Transitions Are Part of Recovery

Workplace recovery does not only happen after work. Brief pauses during the day can give the muscles, eyes, attention, and nervous system a chance to change demand.

OSHA encourages frequent short recovery pauses during computer work, including looking away, stretching, standing, or walking.

Two Minutes Is Better Than Zero

A small pause that fits your schedule is more useful than waiting for the perfect break that never arrives.

What Back-to-Back Meetings Ask of the Body

Meeting fatigue is both mental and physical.

Fixed Screen Attention

The head and eyes may remain directed toward one screen or camera for extended periods.

Limited Position Changes

Conversation, note-taking, and camera expectations may reduce normal shifting and movement.

Shoulder and Jaw Tension

Concentration, stress, speaking, and time pressure may increase bracing through the jaw, neck, and shoulders.

Reduced Breathing Variety

Speaking, listening closely, or feeling anxious may change breathing rhythm and increase upper-body effort.

Emotional Carryover

A difficult conversation may remain in the body even after the meeting window closes.

No Time to Reset the Workspace

Notes, devices, posture, and clutter may carry directly into the next task without adjustment.

Your Two-Minute Meeting Reset

Use all four parts, or choose the parts that fit your space and mobility needs.

0–30 Seconds

Look Away

Take your eyes off the screen. Look across the room or toward a distant object and blink naturally.

30–60 Seconds

Stand or Shift

Stand, walk a few steps, or change your seated position if standing is not comfortable or practical.

60–90 Seconds

Move the Upper Body

Roll the shoulders, turn the head comfortably, open and close the hands, and let the arms move.

90–120 Seconds

Exhale and Reorganize

Allow one or two slow exhalations, release the jaw, reset the screen and notes, and identify the next task.

Adapt the Reset to the Meeting You Just Had

Different meetings create different kinds of fatigue.

After a Long Presentation

Prioritize walking, changing visual distance, and moving the neck, shoulders, and spine.

After a Difficult Conversation

Focus on unclenching the jaw, lowering the shoulders, breathing comfortably, and naming the next practical step.

After Heavy Note-Taking

Release the pen or keyboard, open the hands, move the wrists, and let the forearms rest.

After a Camera-On Meeting

Move out of the camera-facing position and allow natural shifting, stretching, and facial relaxation.

Before a High-Stakes Meeting

Use the reset to check the chair, screen, notes, water, breathing, and physical comfort before beginning.

When You Cannot Leave the Desk

Change seated position, move the ankles and shoulders, look away, and release unnecessary gripping.

Create Space Before the Calendar Fills It

  • Schedule 25- or 50-minute meetings when you control the invitation.
  • Block brief transition time after demanding conversations.
  • Avoid using every break to check another screen.
  • Stand or walk during meetings that do not require detailed computer work.
  • Keep water, notes, and frequently used materials within easy reach.
  • Use longer movement and meal breaks in addition to microbreaks.

How Massage Supports Meeting-Heavy Workdays

When repeated meetings leave the neck, shoulders, jaw, back, hips, forearms, or hands feeling overworked, massage can support comfort and recovery.

Release Accumulated Tension

Massage may help reduce muscular guarding that builds through hours of concentration and screen use.

Include the Jaw and Scalp

When appropriate and requested, focused work may support clients who notice clenching or head and neck tension during stressful workdays.

Support Back and Hip Comfort

Therapeutic massage can address areas that feel stiff or fatigued after prolonged sitting.

Address Forearms and Hands

Sessions can include areas affected by note-taking, typing, mouse use, and repetitive device handling.

Restore Body Awareness

A massage session may help you notice patterns of bracing, breath-holding, leaning, or shoulder elevation.

Create a Real Recovery Window

Massage provides dedicated time away from meetings and screens while supporting relaxation and physical comfort.

A Two-Minute Reset Is Not the Entire Recovery Plan

Brief pauses cannot replace meal breaks, regular physical activity, sleep, workload changes, supportive management, medical care, or meaningful time away from work.

They are useful because they interrupt an otherwise continuous demand and create a small opportunity to respond earlier.

When to Seek Medical Care

Persistent or worsening pain, weakness, numbness, severe headaches, dizziness, chest pain, shortness of breath, symptoms after an injury, or discomfort that interferes with normal work and daily activity should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Your body should not have to wait until the end of the workday for every form of recovery.

Small transitions can reduce the buildup of stiffness and help you notice tension before it becomes overwhelming.

Massage can complement those daily resets by providing deeper, dedicated support for comfort and recovery.
