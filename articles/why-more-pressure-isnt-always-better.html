Why More Pressure Isn't Always Better

Understanding why effective massage is about working with your body—not against it.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 5-minute read

Many people believe that the deeper a massage hurts, the more effective it must be. It's a common belief—but it's not always true.

While firm pressure can be beneficial in certain situations, therapeutic massage is not a contest to see how much discomfort you can tolerate.

In many cases, excessive pressure can actually make it harder for your muscles to relax. The goal of massage isn't to create pain. The goal is to help your body move, recover, and function more comfortably.

Pain Is Not the Goal

Some tenderness during massage can be completely normal, especially when working through areas of chronic tension.

However, there is an important difference between:

•Therapeutic discomfort

•Sharp or overwhelming pain

Therapeutic discomfort often feels like "good pressure"—noticeable but manageable.

Pain that causes you to tense up, hold your breath, or pull away is usually a sign that the pressure should be adjusted.

Your Muscles Are Designed to Protect You

Your nervous system constantly monitors your body for potential threats. If pressure becomes too intense, your muscles may respond by tightening instead of relaxing.

This protective response, called muscle guarding, is completely normal. Instead of helping the therapist reach deeper tissues, excessive pressure may actually make those tissues more resistant to treatment.

Relaxed Muscles Respond Better

When your body feels safe, muscles are more likely to::

  • Relax naturally
  • Improve circulation
  • Allow better movement
  • Respond to therapeutic techniques
  • Recover more comfortably afterward
Sometimes less pressure produces better long-term results.

Everyone Has a Different Comfort Level

Pressure is highly personal.

Factors that influence what feels appropriate include:

  • Previous injuries
  • Daily stress
  • Sleep Quality
  • Hydration
  • Activity Level
  • Medical conditions
  • Personal preference

The "right" pressure isn't determined by the therapist—it's determined by how your body responds.

Communication Is Part of the Treatment

One of the most helpful things you can do during a massage is simply speak up.

Your therapist wants to know if:

  • Pressure feels too intense
  • An area feels especially sensitive
  • You need to change positions
  • You would like more or less pressure
  • Something doesn't feel right

Good communication helps create a safer and more effective session.

The FlowTherapy Philosophy

At FlowTherapy, every massage is individualized.

Rather than trying to deliver the deepest massage possible, the focus is on finding the pressure that allows your body to relax while effectively addressing your goals.

Sometimes that's lighter pressure.

Sometimes it's deeper.

Most often, it's somewhere in between.

The best massage isn't the one that hurts the most—it's the one that helps you move, feel, and function better afterward.

Did You Know?

Feeling sore for a day or two after a therapeutic massage can be normal, but excessive bruising or severe pain is not an expected part of effective massage therapy. If a session leaves you feeling worse rather than better, let your therapist know so future treatments can be adjusted.
