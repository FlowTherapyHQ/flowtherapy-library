Why Desk Work Can Cause Neck and Shoulder Tension

Understanding how modern work affects your neck, shoulders, forearms, wrists, and hands—and what you can do about it.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 8–9 minute read

If you spend most of your day working at a computer, you may be familiar with tight shoulders, neck stiffness, tired forearms, or aching wrists and hands by the end of the day.

These sensations are common among office workers, remote employees, hybrid workers, students, business owners, and anyone who spends extended periods typing, clicking, scrolling, or looking at a screen.

Why Do I Hurt After Sitting All Day?

Desk work may not appear physically demanding, but your muscles are still working. The muscles of your neck and upper back help support your head. Your shoulders stabilize your arms. Your forearms, wrists, hands, and fingers perform hundreds or even thousands of small, repetitive movements.

Many people assume this discomfort is simply part of having a desk job. Although work-related tension is common, understanding why it develops can help you make practical changes that may improve comfort and support healthier work habits.

The issue is not necessarily sitting itself. Your body can tolerate many different positions. Discomfort is more likely to develop when you remain in one position for a long time or repeat the same movements without enough variation.

Your Body Was Designed to Move

Muscles naturally alternate between activity and rest as you move throughout the day. When you remain in one position for hours, certain muscles continue supporting your head, shoulders, arms, and spine without receiving much of a break.

Even a posture that initially feels comfortable can become tiring when it is maintained for an extended period. Standing, walking, shifting your weight, changing chairs, or adjusting your position gives your body an opportunity to distribute the work differently.

The best posture is your next posture.

It Is Not Just About “Bad Posture”

Many people have been told that their discomfort is caused by sitting incorrectly. Posture can influence comfort, but there is no single position that every person must maintain throughout the day.

Even an upright, well-supported position may become uncomfortable when it is held for hours without movement. Rather than forcing yourself into a rigid position, aim for comfortable support and frequent changes.

Allow your chair to support you. Keep commonly used equipment within easy reach. Adjust your position before discomfort becomes intense. The goal is not perfection. The goal is variety.

Work-From-Home Reality

Remote work offers flexibility and convenience, but many home workstations were not created for full workdays.

Telecommuters may find themselves working from:

  • Kitchen or dining-room tables
  • Couches and recliners
  • Coffee tables
  • Laptops placed directly on the lap
  • Screens positioned far below eye level

Remote employees may also move less because there is no commute, no walk to a conference room, and fewer reasons to leave the desk between meetings.

A home workspace does not have to be expensive or perfect. It should allow you to sit comfortably, reach your keyboard and mouse without straining, view your screen without constantly looking downward, and change positions regularly.

Your Neck Is Supporting More Than You Realize

Your head is supported by muscles throughout the neck, shoulders, upper back, and chest. When a screen is too low or too far away, you may gradually move your head forward or look downward for long periods.

Briefly looking down is not inherently dangerous. The concern is the combination of repetition, duration, and limited movement. Holding your head in one position while concentrating may leave the supporting muscles feeling tired, stiff, or overworked.

Laptop users may notice this more because the keyboard and screen are attached. Raising the laptop can improve the height of the screen but may make the keyboard difficult to reach. A separate keyboard and mouse may allow more comfortable placement.

Your Shoulders Stabilize Your Arms

Your shoulders do more than move your arms. They also help stabilize them while you type, use a mouse, write, hold a phone, or reach for objects on your desk.

If your keyboard or mouse is too far away, you may reach forward continuously. If your work surface is too high, you may unconsciously lift your shoulders. During stressful tasks, your shoulders may also begin creeping toward your ears.

These habits can increase the amount of work required from the neck, shoulder, chest, and upper-back muscles. Those areas may eventually feel tight, tender, tired, or difficult to relax.

Do Not Overlook Your Forearms, Wrists, and Hands

Desk-related tension does not stop at the shoulders. Your forearms, wrists, hands, and fingers perform a significant amount of repetitive work throughout the day.

The forearm contains numerous muscles that control movement of the wrists, hands, and fingers. These muscles are active whenever you type, click, scroll, write, grip a mouse, use a touchpad, or hold a phone.

Each movement may require very little effort, but repeating those movements for hours may contribute to fatigue, tenderness, stiffness, or a persistent sensation of tightness.

Wrist position also matters. Working with the wrists repeatedly bent upward, downward, or toward one side may become uncomfortable. A firm mouse grip may create additional demand through the hand and forearm.

Many people request massage for the neck and shoulders without realizing how much tension they are also carrying through the forearms, wrists, and hands.

Your neck and shoulders may feel the tension, but your hands and forearms may be doing much of the work.

Stress Can Make Tension More Noticeable

Desk work is not only physically demanding. Deadlines, meetings, emails, multitasking, and constant notifications can also place mental demands on the body.

During stressful or highly focused tasks, you may unconsciously:

  • Raise your shoulders
  • Clench your jaw
  • Grip the mouse tightly
  • Press forcefully against the keyboard
  • Hold your breath
  • Remain motionless for longer than usual

Stress does not explain every instance of muscle pain. However, it may increase muscle activity, reduce movement, and make existing discomfort feel more noticeable.

Simple Workspace Changes That May Help

You do not need to purchase an entirely new office to make useful changes. Start with the equipment you already have and focus on comfort, support, and movement.

  • Position your screen so you can view it without constantly looking sharply downward.
  • Keep your keyboard and mouse close enough that you do not need to reach forward continuously.
  • Allow your shoulders to remain relaxed rather than elevated.
  • Support your feet with the floor, a stable box, or a footrest.
  • Avoid pressing your wrists firmly against a sharp desk edge.
  • Relax your grip on the mouse and use only the force needed to type.
  • Consider using a separate keyboard and mouse with your laptop.
  • Change positions before discomfort becomes intense.

The 30-Second Desk Reset

Try this brief reset several times throughout your workday. Every movement should remain gentle and comfortable.

  1. Release your shoulders. Let them move away from your ears and roll them backward several times.
  2. Move your hands. Slowly open and close your hands approximately ten times.
  3. Circle your wrists. Make a few comfortable circles in each direction.
  4. Reset your head position. Gently draw your chin backward without forcing it downward.
  5. Stand and walk. Leave your chair for at least 30 seconds.
  6. Take a slow breath. Relax your jaw, hands, and shoulders before returning to work.

This routine is not intended to treat an injury or eliminate every ache. Its purpose is to interrupt prolonged sitting and repetitive activity by giving your body a brief change in position.

How Massage May Help

Therapeutic massage may help reduce the sensation of muscle tightness, encourage relaxation, improve comfort, and support easier movement.

For clients who spend long hours working at a computer, treatment may include more than the neck alone. Depending on the client’s needs and consent, a session may also address the:

  • Shoulders
  • Upper back
  • Chest
  • Upper arms
  • Forearms
  • Wrists
  • Hands

Many clients are surprised by how much forearm and hand tension they notice once those areas receive focused attention.

Massage cannot correct an unsuitable workstation and should not replace medical care. It is best viewed as one part of a broader approach that may include regular movement, appropriate exercise, adequate sleep, stress management, and practical workspace changes.

When to Seek Medical Care

Not every symptom related to computer work is routine muscle tension. Contact a qualified healthcare provider if you experience persistent or worsening numbness or tingling, progressive weakness, reduced coordination, loss of grip strength, frequently dropping objects, severe or steadily worsening pain, significant swelling, or symptoms following an injury.

Seek prompt medical care for chest pain, difficulty breathing, sudden unexplained pain, or other symptoms that may indicate an emergency. Massage should not be used to delay appropriate medical evaluation.

Did You Know?

People often move less when working remotely because they no longer walk between offices, conference rooms, parking areas, or public transportation. Intentional movement breaks can help replace some of the activity that naturally disappears when the commute does.
