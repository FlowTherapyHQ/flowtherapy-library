Building a Better Workstation

A practical guide to monitor placement, keyboard and mouse position, chair support, lighting, reach, movement, and recovery.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 9-minute read

A better workstation is not necessarily the most expensive workstation.

Small changes in screen position, reach distance, chair support, lighting, and task variety can reduce unnecessary effort during the day.

The goal is not to freeze the body into one perfect posture. The goal is to make work more comfortable and make position changes easier.

Fit the Workstation to the Person

OSHA notes that there is no single correct posture or equipment arrangement that fits everyone.

Your height, vision, task demands, mobility, equipment, and available space all influence what feels supportive.

Start With the Biggest Irritation

You do not need to redesign everything at once. Fix the problem that causes the most repeated reaching, leaning, squinting, pressure, or discomfort.

Your Workstation Audit

Use these areas to identify where the setup may be asking your body to work harder than necessary.

Monitor

Place the main screen directly in front of you and at a distance that allows easy reading without leaning forward.

Keyboard

Keep it directly in front of you, with the shoulders relaxed, elbows near the body, and wrists in line with the forearms.

Mouse or Pointer

Keep the pointing device close to the keyboard so you do not repeatedly reach or hold the arm away from the body.

Chair

Use a stable chair that supports the back and allows the feet to rest comfortably on the floor or a foot support.

Work Surface

Keep frequently used items within easy reach and allow enough space for both the keyboard and mouse.

Lighting and Vision

Reduce glare, adjust text size, and use lighting that lets you see without squinting or craning toward the screen.

Phone and Documents

Use a headset for frequent calls and place documents where they can be read without repeated twisting or looking sharply downward.

Movement Space

Leave enough room to stand, shift position, stretch, and walk away from the workstation.

Fix These Problems First

These changes often provide more value than buying multiple accessories without a plan.

Repeated Reaching

Move the Mouse, Phone, and Supplies Closer

Repeated reaching can increase effort through the shoulder, upper back, arm, and forearm.

Leaning Forward

Improve Screen Visibility

Increase text size, reduce glare, adjust monitor distance, and address vision needs before repeatedly pulling the head toward the screen.

Unsupported Feet

Lower the Chair or Add a Foot Support

Stable foot support can reduce pressure and make it easier to use the chair back.

Elevated Shoulders

Lower the Input Devices

A keyboard or mouse that is too high may encourage the shoulders to stay lifted during work.

Laptop-Only Setup

Separate the Screen From the Keyboard

For longer sessions, use a laptop stand or external monitor with a separate keyboard and mouse when possible.

A Better Workstation in Five Practical Changes

Make one change, use it for several days, and adjust based on comfort and task demands.

1. Center the Main Screen

Place the primary monitor where the head, neck, and torso can face forward.

2. Bring Work Within Reach

Keep the keyboard, mouse, phone, and frequently used materials close.

3. Support the Feet and Back

Adjust the chair or use a foot support so the body feels stable rather than suspended.

4. Improve Visibility

Adjust lighting, glare, screen distance, and text size before changing posture to compensate.

5. Build Movement Into the Work Process

Use short recovery pauses to look away, stand, stretch, walk, or complete a different task.

How Massage Supports a Better Workstation Plan

Even a thoughtfully arranged workstation cannot eliminate every source of fatigue, stress, repetitive use, or muscular tension.

Neck and Shoulder Recovery

Massage may help reduce tension associated with screen work, concentration, and sustained arm positions.

Forearm and Hand Support

Focused work may help when typing, mouse use, gripping, or repetitive tasks leave the forearms and hands feeling overworked.

Back and Hip Comfort

Therapeutic massage can include the back, glutes, and hips after long periods of sitting or standing.

Body Awareness

A session may help you notice where you brace, hold your breath, elevate the shoulders, or shift unevenly.

Movement Readiness

When muscular guarding decreases, walking, stretching, changing positions, and exercise may feel easier.

Consistent Maintenance

Regular massage can support a proactive workplace wellness routine instead of waiting until discomfort becomes severe.

What Equipment Cannot Solve

No chair, keyboard, desk, or monitor can replace regular movement, sleep, exercise, recovery, and appropriate medical care.

Equipment should support the task. It should not become another reason to remain in one position for hours.

When to Seek Medical Care

Persistent or worsening pain, weakness, numbness, loss of coordination, severe headaches, symptoms after an injury, or discomfort that interferes with sleep, work, or daily activity should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

A better workstation reduces unnecessary effort and gives your body more options.

Ergonomic changes, regular movement, and therapeutic massage can work together to support comfort and recovery.

The goal is not a perfect-looking posture. The goal is a workspace your body can use more comfortably.
