Why Walking Is Underrated

Why one of the simplest forms of movement can support endurance, mobility, mood, sleep, balance, and everyday recovery.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Walking is so familiar that it is easy to overlook as exercise.

It does not require a complicated program, expensive equipment, or a high-intensity workout. It can be adapted to different speeds, distances, surfaces, schedules, and ability levels.

For many people, walking is one of the most practical ways to build regular movement into daily life.

Walking Counts

Brisk walking is a moderate-intensity aerobic activity and can contribute toward the weekly physical activity recommended for adults.

Slower walking can also be valuable for mobility, recovery, confidence, circulation, and reducing long periods of inactivity.

You Do Not Need 10,000 Steps to Begin

A round number can be motivating, but it is not a universal requirement. Start from your current level and gradually build a realistic amount of walking.

What Walking Can Support

Walking affects more than the legs. It challenges several systems at once in a way that can be easy to scale.

Heart and Lungs

Aerobic Endurance

A brisk pace can raise heart rate and breathing enough to build cardiovascular fitness over time.

Muscles and Joints

Repeated, Functional Movement

Walking uses the hips, knees, ankles, feet, trunk, and arms in a familiar pattern that supports everyday movement capacity.

Balance and Coordination

Confidence With Movement

Each step requires weight transfer, timing, balance, and coordination between the trunk and limbs.

Mood and Stress

A Change of Environment

Walking may support mood, reduce stress, provide mental space, and create a break from screens or repetitive tasks.

Sleep and Energy

Regular Daily Activity

Physical activity can support sleep quality, daily function, and overall energy.

Not Every Walk Has the Same Goal

Pace and purpose can change from day to day.

Recovery Walk

Easy and Comfortable

Use a relaxed pace after a long workday, travel, exercise, or massage when comfortable movement feels helpful.

Everyday Walk

Steady and Sustainable

Walk at a pace that feels purposeful but still allows conversation.

Fitness Walk

Brisk and More Demanding

Increase pace, hills, distance, or duration gradually when the goal is to challenge aerobic fitness.

Walking Can Be Spread Across the Day

One long walk is not the only way to accumulate movement.

Morning

Start With Five to Ten Minutes

Use a short walk to loosen up, get outside, or transition into the day.

Midday

Break Up Sitting

Walk after lunch, between appointments, or during a phone call.

Afternoon

Reset Energy and Attention

A short walk may help reduce stiffness and provide a mental break.

Evening

Use a Comfortable Wind-Down Pace

Choose a safe route and a pace that supports relaxation rather than leaving you overstimulated before bed.

When Walking Feels Hard to Start

  • Begin with two to five minutes rather than waiting for a full workout window.
  • Use a hallway, indoor public space, treadmill, or seated stepping when weather or safety is a concern.
  • Choose flatter surfaces and supportive footwear when hills or uneven ground increase symptoms.
  • Walk with another person when company improves safety, confidence, or consistency.
  • Use a mobility aid when it has been recommended and helps you move more safely.
  • Schedule supportive massage or rehabilitation when pain and muscular tension keep interrupting the routine.

How Massage Can Support a Walking Routine

Massage can help make regular walking feel more comfortable by supporting the muscles and soft tissues that repeatedly work during each step.

Ease Leg and Hip Tension

Therapeutic massage may help the calves, thighs, glutes, hips, and lower back feel less tight or overworked.

Support Recovery Between Walks

Massage can complement sleep, hydration, nutrition, gradual training, and comfortable movement after increased activity.

Improve Movement Readiness

When muscular guarding decreases, walking may feel easier to begin and more comfortable to continue.

Address Foot and Lower-Leg Fatigue

Focused work through the feet, ankles, and calves may be useful when those areas feel tired after long periods of standing or walking.

Support Body Awareness

A session may help you notice gripping, breath-holding, uneven weight shifting, or areas that consistently work harder.

Encourage Consistent Care

Regular massage can support a sustainable movement routine rather than waiting until discomfort stops activity completely.

Build a Walking Plan That Lasts

Progress does not require a dramatic starting point.

Start

Use Your Current Baseline

Choose a distance or duration you can complete without causing a major symptom flare.

Repeat

Walk Often Enough to Build Familiarity

A smaller amount repeated consistently can be more useful than an occasional long walk.

Progress

Change One Variable at a Time

Increase time, pace, hills, or distance gradually rather than changing everything at once.

Recover

Plan for Rest and Support

Use sleep, food, hydration, massage, and easier days to support adaptation.

Adjust

Respond to the Day You Are Having

Pain, fatigue, illness, heat, stress, and schedule may require a shorter or slower walk.

Expand

Add Strength and Balance Work

Walking is valuable, but adults also benefit from muscle-strengthening activity, and older adults benefit from balance work.

What Walking Cannot Do Alone

Walking is a strong foundation, but it does not replace all forms of exercise. Strength, balance, mobility, and activity-specific training may also be important.

Walking also cannot diagnose the cause of pain or replace medical evaluation when symptoms are persistent, severe, or worsening.

When to Stop and Seek Medical Care

Stop walking and seek prompt evaluation for chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, sudden weakness or numbness, severe dizziness, inability to bear weight, rapidly increasing swelling, calf pain with one-sided swelling, or symptoms after a major fall or injury. Persistent pain, balance problems, or breathlessness that limits normal walking should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Walking does not need to look impressive to be valuable.

It can support mobility, endurance, mood, recovery, and confidence while fitting into ordinary life.

Massage can help reduce tension and support recovery so walking feels more available, comfortable, and sustainable.
