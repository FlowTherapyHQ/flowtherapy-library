Understanding the ACL, PCL, MCL & LCL

The knee’s four major ligaments help guide and stabilize the joint in different directions. Learn where each ligament is located, how injuries may occur, which symptoms need medical attention, and where massage may fit during medically guided recovery.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT August 2026 Approximately 10-minute read

The quick difference

The ACL and PCL cross inside the knee and help control forward and backward movement of the shin bone. The MCL and LCL run along the inner and outer sides of the knee and help resist excessive side-to-side movement. Injury may involve stretching, a partial tear, or a complete tear.

What do knee ligaments do?

Ligaments are strong bands of connective tissue that connect one bone to another. At the knee, they help keep the thigh bone and shin bone aligned while allowing the joint to bend, straighten, rotate slightly, bear weight, and respond to changing movement demands.

The knee does not rely on one ligament alone. Stability comes from the combined work of the ligaments, joint surfaces, menisci, muscles, tendons, nervous system, and movement strategy.

One injury can involve more than one structure

A forceful twist, collision, fall, or dislocation may affect a ligament together with the meniscus, cartilage, bone, tendon, muscle, nerve, or another ligament. Symptoms alone cannot confirm which structure is injured.

The four major knee ligaments

Inside the joint

ACL: Anterior Cruciate Ligament

The ACL crosses through the center of the knee and helps limit excessive forward movement and rotation of the shin bone relative to the thigh bone.

Often stressed by: sudden deceleration, pivoting, cutting, awkward landing, rapid direction changes, or a force that shifts the knee.

Inside the joint

PCL: Posterior Cruciate Ligament

The PCL also crosses through the center of the knee and helps limit excessive backward movement of the shin bone relative to the thigh bone.

Often stressed by: a force to the front of a bent knee, a dashboard-type impact, a hard fall onto a flexed knee, or severe hyperextension.

Inside of knee

MCL: Medial Collateral Ligament

The MCL runs along the inner side of the knee and helps resist forces that push the knee inward relative to the lower leg.

Often stressed by: a force to the outside of the knee, contact sports, awkward side loading, or a fall that drives the knee inward.

Outside of knee

LCL: Lateral Collateral Ligament

The LCL runs along the outer side of the knee and helps resist forces that push the knee outward relative to the lower leg.

Often stressed by: a force to the inside of the knee, substantial side loading, or a complex injury involving the outer knee structures.

Side-by-side comparison

Ligament General location Primary stabilizing role Common injury mechanism
ACL Center of the knee Helps control forward translation and rotational instability Pivoting, cutting, sudden stopping, awkward landing, or direct trauma
PCL Center of the knee, behind the ACL Helps control backward translation of the shin bone Impact to the front of a bent knee or a fall onto a flexed knee
MCL Inner side of the knee Helps resist excessive inward collapse or side stress Force to the outer knee or substantial inward loading
LCL Outer side of the knee Helps resist excessive outward movement or side stress Force to the inner knee or substantial outward loading

What does a ligament sprain or tear mean?

Clinicians may describe a ligament injury by grade. The grade reflects the amount of tissue damage and resulting looseness or instability, but the exact meaning can vary somewhat by ligament, examination findings, and imaging.

1

Mild sprain

The ligament has been stretched or mildly injured, but it continues to provide substantial stability.

2

Partial tear

More ligament fibers are injured, and the joint may have increased looseness, pain, swelling, or functional limitation.

3

Complete tear

The ligament is torn through or no longer provides normal restraint, which may create substantial instability.

The grade cannot be determined by massage

Determining whether a ligament is stretched, partially torn, or completely torn requires an appropriate medical history, examination, and sometimes imaging. A massage therapist cannot confirm the injured structure or grade the injury.

Possible signs of a knee ligament injury

Symptoms vary according to the ligament involved, severity, associated injuries, and how recently the injury occurred.

  • A popping sensation or sound at the time of injury.
  • Rapid or increasing swelling around the knee.
  • Pain along the inner, outer, front, back, or center of the knee.
  • Difficulty bearing weight, walking, or using stairs.
  • A feeling that the knee may buckle, shift, or give way.
  • Reduced range of motion or difficulty fully bending or straightening the knee.
  • Bruising, tenderness, or substantial sensitivity.
  • Symptoms following a fall, collision, pivot, awkward landing, or sudden change in direction.

Similar symptoms may also occur with fractures, meniscus tears, patellar injuries, tendon injuries, dislocations, cartilage damage, muscle strains, or other conditions.

Can massage help after a knee ligament injury?

Massage does not reconnect torn ligament fibers, restore mechanical stability, or replace rehabilitation. When medical evaluation has occurred and massage is appropriate for the person’s stage of recovery, it may support comfort in surrounding soft tissues.

Surrounding muscle tension

Guarding, bracing, limping, altered walking, and reduced activity can increase tension in the thighs, calves, hips, glutes, or lower back.

General relaxation

Comfortable bodywork may reduce the sense of overall muscle guarding and help a person relax during a stressful recovery.

Comfort during later recovery

With appropriate clearance, massage may complement medically directed rehabilitation by addressing noninjured tissues that feel tight or overworked.

Compensating areas

A therapist may work with the hips, opposite leg, back, feet, or upper body when those areas are uncomfortable from crutch use or changed movement patterns.

What massage cannot do

Massage cannot diagnose the injury

Pain location alone cannot reliably identify whether the ACL, PCL, MCL, LCL, meniscus, bone, tendon, or another structure is involved.

Massage cannot repair a torn ligament

External pressure cannot reconnect a partial or complete ligament tear or restore the ligament’s structural stability.

Massage cannot replace rehabilitation

Strength, coordination, balance, range of motion, and confidence must be restored through an appropriate rehabilitation plan.

Massage cannot make an unstable knee safe

A knee that buckles or shifts requires medical assessment. Symptom relief does not prove that the ligament has healed.

Deep pressure is not automatically therapeutic

Vigorous massage directly over a recently injured, swollen, bruised, surgically repaired, or unstable knee may be inappropriate. Pressure should always reflect the diagnosis, healing stage, medical restrictions, and individual response.

When massage should wait

Direct work around the knee should generally be postponed until serious injury has been evaluated and the person’s care plan is understood.

  • The injury is recent and has not been medically evaluated.
  • There is rapid swelling, extensive bruising, redness, or heat.
  • The person cannot bear weight or the knee repeatedly gives way.
  • A fracture, dislocation, blood clot, infection, or significant tear is suspected.
  • The knee has been immobilized and massage has not been cleared.
  • Surgery was recent or postoperative precautions are still in place.
  • There is an open wound, active incision, drainage, or irritated skin.
  • Calf swelling, unexplained calf pain, chest pain, or shortness of breath raises concern for a possible blood clot.

Why medically guided rehabilitation matters

The appropriate treatment depends on the ligament involved, severity of injury, degree of instability, associated damage, activity goals, age, health history, and whether surgery is recommended.

Protect the healing structure

Early care may include activity modification, bracing, crutches, swelling management, or movement restrictions provided by the treating clinician.

Restore comfortable motion

Rehabilitation may gradually address knee bending, straightening, patellar movement, and mobility in the hip, ankle, or surrounding tissues.

Rebuild strength

The quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes, calves, hips, and trunk may all contribute to controlling the knee during daily and athletic movement.

Restore balance and coordination

The nervous system must regain confidence and control during standing, stepping, landing, turning, and changing direction.

Progress activity gradually

Returning too quickly to running, jumping, pivoting, work, or sport may exceed the knee’s current capacity.

Massage may complement—not direct—the plan

When massage is medically appropriate, it should respect the rehabilitation plan rather than introduce pressure, stretching, or movement that conflicts with current precautions.

When to seek medical care

Arrange prompt evaluation after significant trauma

Seek medical care for a knee injury involving a pop, rapid swelling, visible deformity, inability to bear weight, severe pain, locking, repeated giving way, loss of motion, numbness, unusual foot color or temperature, or symptoms following a collision, fall, forceful twist, or dislocation.

Emergency care may be necessary for an obviously deformed knee, loss of circulation or sensation, an open injury, severe trauma, chest pain, shortness of breath, or symptoms that may indicate a blood clot.
