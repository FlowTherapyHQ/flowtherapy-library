How to Communicate With Your Massage Therapist

Practical language for discussing pressure, comfort, boundaries, preferences, health changes, and what you want from your session.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Many people worry about saying the wrong thing during a massage. They may tolerate uncomfortable pressure, avoid mentioning an area they do not want touched, or remain silent because they do not want to interrupt the therapist.

Clear communication is not an interruption. It is part of the session.

Your massage therapist can make better decisions when they understand your goals, comfort level, health history, boundaries, and response to the techniques being used.

You Are Not Being Difficult

Asking for less pressure, more support, a different position, quieter conversation, another area of focus, or an end to the session is not rude. Massage should remain collaborative, and your comfort and consent can change at any time.

Communication Begins Before the Massage

The conversation before your session helps your therapist plan appropriate care.

You do not need to explain every detail of your life. Share the information that may affect the session, including the reason you booked, areas of concern, areas you want avoided, recent changes in your health, and anything that may affect your comfort.

A therapist may ask follow-up questions to clarify your goals or determine whether massage should be modified, postponed, or coordinated with another healthcare provider.

Communication Happens Throughout the Session

You do not have only one opportunity to speak.

Before

Plan the Session

Discuss goals, pressure preferences, health information, positioning, boundaries, and the areas you would like addressed.

During

Adjust in Real Time

Tell your therapist when pressure, temperature, position, draping, sound, scent, or conversation needs to change.

After

Review Your Response

Share what felt useful, what did not, and anything you would prefer to change during a future appointment.

A massage therapist can feel muscle tension, but they cannot automatically know your pain level, comfort, boundaries, or personal preferences.

What Should You Tell Your Massage Therapist?

Simple, direct language is enough. You do not need to use technical terms.

“The main reason I booked is…”

Explain whether your goal is relaxation, general wellness, focused therapeutic work, exercise recovery, or another need.

“The area bothering me most is…”

Describe the location and whether the sensation feels dull, stiff, tender, sharp, numb, burning, or different from usual.

“Please avoid…”

Name any body areas, techniques, positions, scents, products, or topics of conversation you do not want included.

“I am comfortable with…”

Share your preference for pressure, draping, conversation, music, lighting, temperature, and positioning.

“Since my last session…”

Mention new injuries, procedures, diagnoses, medications, pregnancy, skin changes, swelling, illness, or other health developments.

“I am not sure whether massage is appropriate because…”

Ask questions when symptoms are unusual, unexplained, severe, or connected to a medical concern.

How Should You Describe Pressure?

Terms such as light, medium, firm, and deep can mean different things to different people.

Instead of relying only on labels, describe how the pressure feels and what happens in your body.

Useful feedback includes whether you can breathe normally, remain relaxed, and allow the therapist to work without bracing or pulling away.

Useful Phrases During the Massage

You can say as much or as little as needed.

When You Want Less Pressure

“Please use less pressure here.” “That is becoming too intense.” “I am starting to tense up.” “Could we use a gentler technique?”

When You Want More Pressure

“This area can tolerate a little more pressure.” “Could you gradually increase the pressure?” “That feels comfortable, but I would like slightly firmer work.”

When the Sensation Feels Wrong

“That feels sharp.” “I feel tingling.” “The sensation is traveling down my arm.” “Please stop working there.”

When the Pressure Feels Right

“That feels effective and manageable.” “This pressure is comfortable.” “Please keep the pressure around this level.”

Pain Is Not Required for Effective Massage

Some therapeutic techniques create noticeable pressure or temporary tenderness. That does not mean you must tolerate pain to receive a benefit.

Overwhelming pressure may cause guarding, breath-holding, or increased sensitivity. It can also make it difficult for you to distinguish productive sensation from a warning sign.

Speak up before the pressure becomes unbearable. Your therapist should respond by adjusting the pressure, technique, position, or treatment plan.

Consent Is Ongoing

Agreeing to a massage appointment does not mean agreeing to every technique, body area, position, or level of pressure.

You May Change Your Mind

You can approve an area initially and later decide that you no longer want it addressed.

You May Decline Any Area

You do not have to justify why you prefer that an area not be touched.

You May Pause the Session

You can ask for time to reposition, use the restroom, take a break, or discuss what is happening.

You May End the Session

You may stop the massage at any time when you feel unsafe, unwell, uncomfortable, or simply ready to end.

What Should You Know About Draping?

Professional draping helps protect privacy and maintain clear boundaries. Typically, only the area being massaged is uncovered, while the rest of the body remains covered.

You choose how much clothing to remove based on your comfort and the type of session. You may remain fully clothed when appropriate or leave on any clothing that helps you feel secure.

Tell your therapist when a drape feels loose, exposes more than you expected, or needs to be adjusted.

You Can Adjust More Than Pressure

Small changes can significantly improve your ability to relax and feel safe.

1

Positioning

Ask to turn over, sit up, remain side-lying, use additional pillows, or avoid a position that causes discomfort.

2

Temperature

Request another blanket, less heat, a table-temperature adjustment, or a change to hot towels or stones.

3

Conversation

Let your therapist know whether you prefer quiet, occasional check-ins, or friendly conversation.

4

Music and Sound

Ask for quieter music, different music, reduced volume, or no music when possible.

5

Products and Scents

Mention allergies, sensitivities, skin concerns, or a preference for unscented products.

Your Preferences Matter

Preferences do not have to be medically necessary to be respected.

Quiet or Conversation

You may want silence, reassurance, education, or casual conversation. The preference can change from one session to the next.

Focused or Full-Body Work

Tell your therapist whether you want one concern prioritized or prefer a more general session.

More or Fewer Check-Ins

Some clients appreciate frequent questions. Others prefer fewer interruptions while still knowing they can speak at any time.

Clothing Choices

You may remain fully or partially clothed depending on your comfort and the work being performed.

Areas to Avoid

You may exclude any body area, even when it is near the area causing discomfort.

Technique Preferences

You can decline stretching, percussion, cupping, heat, topical products, or any other enhancement or technique.

Tell Your Therapist When Your Health Changes

Health information helps your therapist determine whether techniques, pressure, positioning, temperature, or the entire session should be modified.

Mention recent surgery, hospitalization, injury, pregnancy, fever, infection, new swelling, skin changes, medication changes, cancer treatment, blood-clot history, fractures, reduced sensation, or another significant change.

Your therapist does not need to diagnose the condition. They do need enough information to decide whether massage is within their scope and appropriate for that day.

What if You Do Not Know How to Describe the Problem?

You do not need to know the name of a muscle, injury, or diagnosis.

Describe what you notice in ordinary language:

  • Where the sensation is located.
  • When it started.
  • What you were doing when it began.
  • What makes it better or worse.
  • Whether it is constant or intermittent.
  • Whether you notice numbness, weakness, swelling, or heat.
  • How it affects movement, sleep, work, or daily activity.

This description is often more useful than trying to guess the diagnosis.

Professional Boundaries Should Be Clear

You should not be expected to manage inappropriate behavior or unclear boundaries during a professional massage.

Touch Should Match the Plan

The therapist should explain sensitive or unexpected areas of work and obtain appropriate permission before proceeding.

Draping Should Protect Privacy

Only the area being treated should be exposed, and the draping should remain secure.

Comments Should Remain Professional

Conversation should not sexualize your body, shame you, or pressure you to disclose personal information.

Your “No” Should Be Respected

A therapist should not argue, persuade, or punish you for declining a technique, area, enhancement, or future appointment.

What if Speaking Up Feels Difficult?

Some clients freeze, become quiet, or struggle to find words when they feel uncomfortable. This can be especially relevant for people with previous trauma, negative healthcare experiences, anxiety, or concerns about disappointing others.

You may communicate before the session by writing preferences on your intake form, sending a message, or asking the therapist to check in at specific points.

You can also agree on simple language such as “pause,” “lighter,” “stop,” or “I need to reposition.”

A trauma-informed therapist should not require you to disclose a trauma history to have your boundaries respected.

Common Communication Myths

Myth: The therapist should already know.

Therapists can observe tension and body language, but they cannot reliably know your pain, comfort, preferences, or emotional response without feedback.

Myth: Speaking interrupts the massage.

Brief communication can prevent discomfort and help the therapist provide a more appropriate session.

Myth: Requesting less pressure wastes the session.

Pressure should match your goals and tolerance. Gentler work can still be therapeutic and effective.

Myth: You must explain every boundary.

“Please do not work there” is complete information. You are not required to provide a personal history to justify the request.

Myth: The original plan cannot change.

The session can be modified when your comfort, symptoms, or preferences change.

What if Your Therapist Does Not Respond Well?

A therapist should listen, clarify when needed, and make reasonable adjustments within their professional scope.

Be cautious when a therapist repeatedly dismisses pain, pressures you to tolerate unwanted touch, ignores your request to stop, discourages medical evaluation, or claims that intense pain is always necessary.

You may end the session, decline future appointments, contact the business, or report conduct to the appropriate licensing authority when necessary.

Communication Continues After the Session

Your response afterward can help improve future care.

What Felt Helpful?

Note the pressure, techniques, positioning, areas of focus, and communication style that worked well.

What Would You Change?

Consider whether you would prefer more or less pressure, different positioning, quieter conversation, or another session focus.

How Did Your Body Respond?

Notice comfort, soreness, movement, sleep, energy, and whether any symptoms worsened.

Does Anything Need Evaluation?

New, severe, persistent, or unusual symptoms may require an appropriate healthcare provider rather than another massage.

Your comfort is not an inconvenience.

Massage is not something being done to you while you remain silent. You are an active participant with the right to ask questions, change the plan, decline touch, pause, or stop.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

At FlowTherapy, communication is part of therapeutic care—not an obstacle to it.

Your preferences may change from day to day. You may want quiet during one session and conversation during another. You may prefer firm pressure in one area and gentle work somewhere else.

The goal is not to make you tolerate a predetermined routine. The goal is to build a session around your current needs, comfort, informed consent, and wellness goals.

When to Seek Medical Care

Massage communication cannot replace medical evaluation. Seek appropriate care for severe or worsening pain, sudden weakness, numbness, significant swelling, fever, unexplained redness or warmth, visible deformity, loss of function, symptoms following a serious injury, chest pain, difficulty breathing, or other concerning changes.

Did You Know?

You do not have to wait until a therapist asks whether the pressure is comfortable. You may give feedback at any point, even when the therapist checked in only moments earlier.
