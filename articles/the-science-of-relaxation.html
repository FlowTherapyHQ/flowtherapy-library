The Science of Relaxation

What relaxation actually is, how the body shifts out of high alert, and why rest takes practice rather than perfection.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Relaxation is often treated like a switch: either you are relaxed or you are not.

In reality, relaxation is a shifting body state. Breathing, heart rate, muscle activity, attention, environment, safety, and emotion can all influence how easily the body settles.

Relaxation is not the absence of responsibility, thought, or movement. It is a reduction in unnecessary effort and threat response.

The Relaxation Response Is a Real Physiological Shift

Relaxation techniques are intended to support the body’s natural relaxation response. This state is commonly associated with slower breathing, a reduced heart rate, lower blood pressure, and a greater sense of calm.

What Happens During Stress?

Stress is the body’s response to a demand, challenge, or perceived threat. The nervous system may increase alertness, redirect attention, change breathing, and increase muscular readiness.

This response is useful when action is needed. The problem is not that the body responds. The problem is when high demand continues without enough opportunity to recover.

Breathing May Speed Up

Breathing can become faster, shallower, or more irregular during periods of stress or urgency.

Muscles May Become More Active

The jaw, shoulders, hands, abdomen, and back may hold more effort than the task actually requires.

Attention May Narrow

The brain may focus more strongly on problems, discomfort, uncertainty, or possible threats.

Recovery May Be Delayed

Sleep, digestion, concentration, and movement habits may be affected when stress remains high.

Stress Response vs. Relaxation Response

These states are not absolute opposites. The body moves along a range depending on context, health, safety, activity, and emotion.

Stress Response
Prepared for action

Alertness, muscular readiness, faster breathing, and increased attention may help the body respond to immediate demands.

Relaxation Response
Reduced unnecessary effort

Breathing may slow, heart rate may decrease, and muscle tension may feel easier to release.

Stress Response
Attention narrows

The mind may focus strongly on deadlines, pain, conflict, or uncertainty.

Relaxation Response
Attention becomes more flexible

The person may notice breathing, body support, surroundings, and choices more clearly.

Relaxation Is Not the Same as Distraction

Watching television, scrolling, gaming, shopping, or staying busy may temporarily redirect attention. That can be enjoyable, but it does not always reduce physical arousal.

A person can be distracted while still clenching the jaw, holding the breath, or feeling mentally overstimulated.

True relaxation usually involves some reduction in demand, effort, vigilance, or sensory load.

What Can Help the Body Settle?

Comfortable Breathing

Slower or more regulated breathing may help some people reduce physical arousal, but it should never be forced.

Progressive Muscle Relaxation

Alternating gentle muscle tension with release may improve awareness of the difference between effort and rest.

Guided Imagery

Directing attention toward calming mental images may help reduce worry or sensory overload.

Gentle Movement

Walking, mobility work, tai chi, or yoga may help some people settle more effectively than sitting still.

Reduced Stimulation

Lower light, quieter sound, fewer screens, and less multitasking may decrease the amount of information the brain must process.

Supportive Touch

Massage may create structured time for physical comfort, reduced effort, and calm when touch feels safe and welcome.

A Five-Minute Relaxation Practice

This is not a performance test. Stop or modify any step that feels uncomfortable.

1

Choose Support

Sit, stand, or lie in a position that does not require more effort than necessary.

2

Notice Contact

Pay attention to the chair, floor, pillows, or other surfaces supporting your body.

3

Reduce One Unnecessary Effort

Let the jaw separate, soften the hands, or allow the shoulders to move without forcing them down.

4

Allow Comfortable Breathing

Let the breath become quieter or slower only if that feels natural. Avoid taking unusually large breaths.

5

Return Gradually

Move your hands or feet, look around the room, and transition back to activity without rushing.

Relaxation Does Not Always Feel Pleasant at First

Quiet, stillness, closed eyes, or body awareness may feel uncomfortable for some people, especially when stress, trauma, pain, dizziness, panic, or sensory sensitivity is present.

Relaxation practices can be adapted. Keeping the eyes open, moving gently, using music, shortening the practice, or choosing a more active form of recovery may feel safer and more useful.

How Can You Tell When Relaxation Is Happening?

Breathing Feels Easier

The breath may become quieter, more regular, or less effortful.

Muscles Use Less Effort

The jaw, hands, shoulders, or abdomen may feel less guarded.

Attention Broadens

You may notice more of the room, your body support, or available choices.

Movement Feels Less Rushed

You may transition between activities with more control and less urgency.

Common Relaxation Myths

Relaxation is a skill and a body state—not a moral achievement.

Myth
Relaxation means doing nothing.

Walking, stretching, creative activity, music, massage, or gentle exercise may be more regulating than complete stillness.

Fact
Different people relax in different ways.

The most useful approach depends on health, history, environment, preference, and current stress level.

Myth
You should be able to relax immediately.

The body may need repeated experiences of safety, support, and reduced demand before settling becomes easier.

Fact
Relaxation techniques are supportive tools.

They may help manage stress symptoms, but they do not replace medical care, psychotherapy, crisis support, or changes to an unsafe environment.

Can Massage Support Relaxation?

Massage may help some people experience reduced muscle tension, slower breathing, greater body awareness, or a temporary sense of calm.

The quality of the experience matters. Pressure, pacing, communication, draping, positioning, temperature, sound, and the ability to say no can all influence whether the body feels supported or guarded.

Massage should not be described as “resetting” the nervous system or permanently switching the body into a relaxed state. It is one supportive experience within a larger wellness routine.

Important Note

Relaxation practices are not a substitute for medical or mental health care. Seek appropriate help when stress, panic, trauma symptoms, depression, sleep problems, substance use, pain, or other symptoms are persistent, severe, worsening, or interfering with daily life. Stop a breathing or relaxation exercise if it causes dizziness, panic, numbness, worsening shortness of breath, or other concerning symptoms.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Relaxation is not something your body owes you on command.

It often develops through repeated experiences of support, choice, comfort, movement, sleep, boundaries, and reduced demand.

Massage can create one of those experiences. The goal is not to force stillness, but to make room for the body to use less effort when it is ready.
