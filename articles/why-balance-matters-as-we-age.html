Movement & Mobility

Why Balance Matters as We Age

How balance supports walking, confidence, independence, and the everyday ability to respond when your body moves unexpectedly.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • August 2026 • 8-minute read

Balance is easy to overlook when it is working well.

You rely on it when standing from a chair, stepping off a curb, turning around, reaching into a cabinet, carrying groceries, climbing stairs, or recovering after an unexpected misstep.

Balance can change with age, but difficulty is not something that must simply be accepted without support. Strength, movement practice, vision care, medication review, appropriate footwear, and safer surroundings may all contribute to better stability.

Balance training does not require standing unsupported on one leg for a long time. It can begin with a stable chair, a countertop, small weight shifts, and movements that match your current ability.

Balance Is a Skill You Can Practice

Balance depends on several systems working together. Although those systems may change over time, appropriate practice can help you strengthen your response, improve confidence, and maintain the movements that support everyday independence.

What Is Balance?

Balance is your ability to control your body’s position while remaining still, moving, or responding to a change in the environment.

It includes keeping your center of mass over your base of support and making quick adjustments when your weight shifts.

Standing quietly is one form of balance. Walking, turning, reaching, stepping around an obstacle, and recovering from a trip require dynamic balance because your body is moving while trying to remain stable.

Your Balance System Works as a Team

Balance is not controlled by one muscle, one joint, or one part of the brain.

Vision

Your Eyes Provide Reference Points

Visual information helps you understand where you are in relation to the floor, walls, furniture, steps, and moving objects around you.

Inner Ear

Your Vestibular System Detects Motion

Structures in the inner ear help detect head movement, acceleration, and changes in orientation.

Body Awareness

Your Joints and Muscles Provide Feedback

Sensory information from the feet, ankles, legs, hips, and other areas helps the nervous system understand body position.

Strength and Control

Your Muscles Create the Response

The body uses strength, coordination, and reaction time to make adjustments when you sway, turn, reach, or take a step.

Good balance does not mean never swaying. It means being able to notice a change and respond with enough control to remain safe.

Balance Is Part of Everyday Movement

You practice balance throughout the day, often without realizing it.

Standing From a Chair

Your body shifts forward, transfers weight into the feet, and stabilizes as you rise.

Walking

Each step briefly places most of your weight over one leg while the other leg moves forward.

Turning

Direction changes require your head, trunk, feet, and hips to coordinate without losing control.

Using Stairs

Stairs require leg strength, foot placement, depth perception, coordination, and weight transfer.

Reaching

Reaching changes your center of mass and requires the body to adjust around the movement.

Stepping Over Obstacles

Curbs, pets, cords, and uneven surfaces require enough foot clearance and control to continue moving safely.

Why Can Balance Change With Age?

Aging itself does not explain every balance concern. Changes often reflect several factors working together.

Muscle strength may decline when activity decreases. Vision or hearing may change. Joint stiffness, foot pain, nerve conditions, medications, illness, dehydration, fear of falling, or previous injuries may also influence stability.

Because balance is multifactorial, improving it may require more than performing one exercise.

Factors That May Affect Balance

A new or worsening balance problem deserves attention rather than being dismissed as “just aging.”

Reduced Leg Strength

Difficulty rising, stepping, or correcting a loss of balance may increase when the legs and hips are less prepared for the task.

Changes in Vision

Reduced visual clarity, depth perception, or contrast can make steps, shadows, and obstacles harder to interpret.

Medication Effects

Some medications may contribute to dizziness, drowsiness, changes in blood pressure, or slower reaction time.

Foot or Joint Problems

Pain, stiffness, reduced sensation, or poorly fitting footwear may change the way you stand and walk.

Inner-Ear Conditions

Vestibular problems may create dizziness, spinning, motion sensitivity, or difficulty stabilizing the head and eyes.

Reduced Activity

Avoiding movement can decrease strength, coordination, and confidence, making ordinary tasks feel less secure.

Neurological Conditions

Stroke, neuropathy, Parkinson’s disease, and other conditions may affect sensation, coordination, strength, or reaction time.

Environmental Hazards

Poor lighting, loose rugs, clutter, uneven surfaces, and lack of support can increase the challenge even when balance is otherwise manageable.

Balance and Confidence Affect Each Other

Fear of falling can lead some people to move less. Reduced activity may then contribute to further losses in strength and confidence.

1

Unsteadiness or a Fall

A difficult experience may create concern about moving safely.

2

Activity Avoidance

Walking, stairs, errands, or social activities may be reduced.

3

Reduced Strength

Less activity may reduce the physical capacity needed for daily movement.

4

Lower Confidence

Movement may begin feeling even more difficult or uncertain.

Falls Are Not an Inevitable Part of Aging

Falls are common among older adults, but common does not mean unavoidable.

Fall risk may be reduced by addressing the factors relevant to the individual. These may include strength and balance exercise, vision care, medication review, safer footwear, home modifications, use of appropriate assistive devices, and treatment of medical conditions.

Tell a healthcare provider about falls, near-falls, dizziness, or new unsteadiness. Even a fall without an obvious injury can provide useful information about changing risk.

Signs That Balance May Need More Attention

These signs do not diagnose a condition, but they may indicate that professional assessment would be useful.

Holding Furniture While Walking

You frequently reach for walls, counters, or furniture to feel stable inside familiar spaces.

Difficulty Turning

Turning requires several cautious steps or creates dizziness, freezing, or fear.

Recent Falls or Near-Falls

You have fallen, tripped frequently, or needed to catch yourself more often than usual.

Avoiding Activities

You have stopped walking outdoors, using stairs, shopping, or attending activities because of balance concerns.

Difficulty Rising

Standing from a chair requires several attempts, heavy use of the arms, or creates significant unsteadiness.

New Dizziness or Weakness

You notice spinning, faintness, sudden weakness, numbness, or a rapid change in walking ability.

A Supported Balance Practice

Practice near a sturdy countertop or heavy chair that will not move. Do not use a rolling chair. Have another person nearby when you are unsure of your safety.

1

Supported Weight Shifts

Stand with your feet comfortably apart and both hands near a stable support. Slowly shift more weight toward one foot, return to center, and shift toward the other foot.

Keep both feet on the floor and make the movement small at first.

Begin with

Five slow shifts in each direction.

Make it easier

Keep both hands firmly on the support and reduce the distance of the shift.

2

Supported Marching

Stand behind a stable chair or at a countertop. Lift one foot slightly, lower it, and repeat on the other side.

The goal is controlled weight transfer—not lifting the knee as high as possible.

Begin with

Five alternating lifts per side.

Make it easier

Lift only the heel or slide one foot forward without fully leaving the floor.

3

Heel Raises

Stand with support and slowly lift both heels while keeping the toes on the floor. Lower with control.

This movement challenges calf strength and your ability to control a changing base of support.

Begin with

Five to eight controlled repetitions.

Make it easier

Use both hands for support and lift the heels only a small amount.

4

Side Steps Along a Counter

Face a countertop and place your hands lightly on the surface. Take several small steps to one side, then return.

Keep your feet facing forward and avoid crossing one foot over the other.

Begin with

Three to five steps in each direction.

Make it easier

Use smaller steps and maintain firm hand support.

5

Staggered Stance

Stand near support and place one foot slightly ahead of the other, as though pausing during a step.

Keep both feet fully supported. Hold briefly, return to a normal stance, and change which foot is forward.

Begin with

Five to ten seconds per side.

Make it easier

Keep the feet wider apart and use both hands for support.

6

Sit to Stand

Sit near the front of a stable chair with your feet supported. Lean forward slightly, press through your feet, and stand.

Pause when stable, then sit down slowly. Use your hands when needed.

Begin with

Three to five controlled repetitions.

Make it easier

Use a higher chair and push from the armrests.

Choose the Right Level of Support

Reducing support is not the only way to progress. Better control, smoother movement, or slightly longer practice can also represent improvement.

Level One

Firm Support

Use both hands on a stable surface and keep your feet in a wide, comfortable position.

Level Two

Light Support

Keep one or both hands available while using only as much pressure as needed.

Level Three

Reduced Support

Practice with fingertips or hands hovering only when you can do so safely and regain support immediately.

Strength Is Part of Balance Training

Balance exercises help your body practice stability, but strength provides the physical capacity to respond.

The ankles, calves, thighs, hips, and trunk all contribute when you rise, step, turn, reach, or recover from a loss of balance.

A complete plan may include both balance-specific practice and strengthening exercises appropriate for your ability.

Movements That Support Balance

These activities can be adapted and performed with support when needed.

Sit to Stand

Builds strength and control for rising from chairs, toilets, beds, and other surfaces.

Heel Raises

Strengthens the calves and practices control as your weight shifts toward the front of the feet.

Side Steps

Challenges the outer hips and prepares the body to move in a direction used less often during ordinary walking.

Supported Marching

Practices transferring weight from one leg to the other while maintaining upright control.

Step Practice

A low step with appropriate support may help prepare for curbs and stairs when approved for your ability.

Walking

Regular walking provides repeated practice with shifting weight, stepping, turning, and responding to the environment.

Why Practice Turning?

Many falls and near-falls occur during transitions rather than while standing still.

Turning requires coordination between the eyes, head, trunk, hips, and feet. Trying to pivot quickly on one planted foot may feel less stable than taking several controlled steps.

Practice turning near support. Look in the direction you intend to go, take small steps, and avoid rushing.

Your Environment Matters Too

Exercise can support balance, but the environment should not create unnecessary challenges.

Improve Lighting

Use adequate lighting near stairs, hallways, bathrooms, and routes used at night.

Clear Walking Paths

Remove or secure cords, clutter, loose rugs, and objects that create tripping hazards.

Use Stable Support

Install appropriate handrails or grab bars rather than relying on towel racks or unstable furniture.

Review Footwear

Wear shoes that fit securely and provide appropriate traction for the environment.

Keep Essentials Accessible

Store frequently used items where they do not require unsafe climbing, reaching, or bending.

Plan for Nighttime Movement

Keep a clear route to the bathroom and allow time for your body to adjust after getting out of bed.

Tell Your Healthcare Provider About Falls

Falls and near-falls provide important information, even when they do not cause an obvious injury.

Your provider may review vision, hearing, blood pressure, medications, walking, footwear, muscle strength, home safety, and health conditions that affect balance.

Do not stop prescription medication on your own. Ask whether any medication or combination of medications may contribute to dizziness, sedation, or changes in blood pressure.

What About Canes and Walkers?

An assistive device can improve safety and confidence when it is appropriate, correctly fitted, and used as intended.

A device that is the wrong height or used incorrectly may create new challenges. A physical therapist, occupational therapist, or other qualified professional can help select and fit the device.

Using assistance is not a failure. The purpose is to support safe movement and continued participation in daily life.

Common Balance Myths

Myth: Falling is a normal part of aging.

Falls become more common with age, but risk factors can often be identified and addressed.

Myth: Balance means standing on one leg.

Balance also includes walking, turning, reaching, changing speed, stepping over objects, and recovering from a misstep.

Myth: Holding furniture is enough exercise.

Furniture walking may indicate reduced confidence or stability and can be unsafe when furniture moves or is poorly positioned.

Myth: Avoiding movement prevents falls.

Excessive avoidance may reduce strength and confidence. The safer approach is appropriately supported movement and professional guidance when needed.

Myth: You must practice without support.

A stable surface allows you to practice safely. Support can be reduced gradually only when appropriate.

Myth: Massage can correct balance problems.

Massage may support comfort, but it does not replace balance assessment, strength training, vision care, vestibular treatment, medication review, or rehabilitation.

Can Massage Support Balance?

Massage may help some people feel more comfortable, relaxed, or willing to move when muscle tension or soreness is limiting activity.

It does not directly train balance reactions, improve vision, treat vestibular conditions, or replace leg-strengthening and walking practice.

Massage should be considered one supportive wellness tool within a broader plan. Tell your therapist about falls, dizziness, neuropathy, weakness, recent injuries, medications, or difficulty changing positions.

Stop and Seek Help When

Mild muscle effort may be expected. Sudden or severe symptoms should not be treated as a normal part of balance practice.

You feel faint or severely dizzy

Sit or lie down safely and seek appropriate care when symptoms are new, severe, or persistent.

You develop sudden weakness or numbness

Sudden neurological symptoms may require urgent medical evaluation.

You experience chest pain

Stop immediately and seek emergency medical care.

You cannot regain stability

Sit safely and do not continue exercises that exceed your current ability.

You fall or sustain an injury

Seek evaluation when you hit your head, cannot bear weight, have severe pain, or take medication that increases bleeding risk.

Your walking changes suddenly

A sudden change in gait, coordination, speech, vision, or facial movement may be a medical emergency.

Using support is part of safe training.

The goal is not to prove that you can stand without help. The goal is to practice movement at a level that challenges you without creating unnecessary danger.

The FlowTherapy Perspective

Balance is not only an exercise category. It supports confidence, independence, and participation in the activities that make up daily life.

A thoughtful plan may include balance practice, leg strength, walking, movement variety, safer surroundings, and medical or rehabilitation support when needed.

Massage can help address comfort and tension, but lasting balance improvements require the body to practice standing, stepping, turning, and responding with appropriate support.

When to Seek Medical Care

Seek medical evaluation for new or worsening balance problems, repeated falls, fainting, severe dizziness, sudden weakness or numbness, changes in speech or vision, new confusion, loss of coordination, severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, or difficulty walking. Call emergency services for possible signs of a stroke, heart problem, or serious injury.

Did You Know?

Balance is most useful when it is practiced in ways that resemble real life. Weight shifting, turning, stepping sideways, rising from a chair, and changing direction may be as important as standing still.
