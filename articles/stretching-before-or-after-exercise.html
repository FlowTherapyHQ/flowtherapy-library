Should You Stretch Before or After Exercise?

The answer depends on the type of activity you're doing. Understanding when and how to stretch may help you move more comfortably and prepare your body for exercise.

By Brandy Hennigan, LMT • July 2026 • 6-minute read

For years, many people were told to stretch before every workout. Today, research paints a more complete picture. Stretching still has value, but the timing and type of stretching matter.

Rather than asking whether stretching is "good" or "bad," a better question is whether you're using the right kind of stretching for the activity you're about to do.

Static vs. Dynamic Stretching

Not all stretching is the same. The two most common types are static stretching and dynamic stretching.

Static stretching involves gently holding a muscle in a lengthened position for a period of time, often 15 to 60 seconds.

Dynamic stretching involves controlled, active movements that gradually take your joints and muscles through their available range of motion without holding the position.

Before Exercise

For many activities, a brief dynamic warm-up may be more beneficial than immediately performing long static stretches. Gentle movements increase circulation, prepare muscles for activity, and allow your nervous system to gradually transition into exercise.

Examples include arm circles, bodyweight squats, walking lunges, leg swings, marching, shoulder rolls, and light movement that resembles the activity you're preparing to perform.

A warm muscle is generally more prepared for movement than a cold muscle.

After Exercise

After physical activity, many people enjoy static stretching because it provides an opportunity to slow down, relax, and gently restore comfortable movement. Stretching after exercise may feel especially beneficial if certain muscles tend to feel tight following activity.

The goal is not to force your body into extreme positions. Stretch until you feel a gentle pull—not pain—and breathe normally throughout the stretch.

Should Everyone Stretch?

Not necessarily. The answer depends on your goals, your activity, your current mobility, previous injuries, and your overall health.

Some people benefit more from strengthening, balance training, or improving movement quality than from spending long periods stretching. A balanced movement program often includes several different elements rather than focusing on only one.

Common Stretching Mistakes

  • Bouncing aggressively during a stretch.
  • Stretching into pain instead of gentle tension.
  • Holding your breath.
  • Ignoring one side of the body.
  • Stretching an injured area without medical guidance.
  • Expecting stretching alone to solve every movement problem.

Where Massage Fits In

Massage and stretching complement one another, but they serve different purposes. Massage may help reduce the sensation of muscle tightness and improve comfort, while movement and exercise help your body maintain strength, coordination, and mobility over time.

For many people, the best long-term approach combines regular movement, appropriate recovery, strength training, and therapeutic bodywork when needed.

When to Seek Medical Care

If stretching consistently causes sharp pain, numbness, dizziness, joint instability, or worsening symptoms, stop the activity and seek evaluation from a qualified healthcare provider. Stretching should not be used to push through a significant injury.

Did You Know?

Flexibility is only one part of healthy movement. Strength, balance, coordination, endurance, and mobility all work together to help your body move efficiently.
